## Membership model

**How the fee works (proven, 10-K FY2025 and FY2026):**
- Gold Star (individual) and Business: $65/year in the U.S. Executive is "an additional annual fee of $65" in the U.S. (so $130 total) and earns a 2% reward on qualified purchases, "generally up to a maximum reward of $1,250 per year" (10-K FY2025).
- "We account for membership fee revenue on a deferred basis, recognized ratably over the one-year membership period." That is why a fee increase on Sep 1, 2024 only showed up gradually over FY2025 and FY2026.
- Costco's own description of the model (10-K): "This format is designed to reinforce member loyalty and provide continuing fee revenue. The extent to which we achieve growth in our membership base, increase the penetration of Executive memberships, and sustain high renewal rates materially influences our profitability."
- Accounting detail worth knowing: "The 2% reward associated with Executive membership reduces net sales" (10-K FY2025). The cost of the Executive perk is booked against merchandise sales, not against membership fees.

**Members (proven, 10-K):**
- Paid members: 76.2M (FY24) -> 81.0M (FY25, +6.3%) -> 84.1M (FY26, +3.8%).
- Executive: 35.4M -> 38.7M -> 42.3M. In FY26 Executive members were about half of paid members (42.3 / 84.1 = 50.3%) but about 75.2% of worldwide net sales.
- Cardholders including free household cards: 150.4M at end of FY26.
- Renewal rates: U.S. & Canada 92.9% (FY24), 92.3% (FY25), 92.3% (FY26); worldwide 90.5%, 89.8%, 89.8%. The rate is "a trailing calculation that captures renewals during the period seven to eighteen months prior to the reporting date."
- Why renewal dipped (10-K FY2025): "Renewal rates were negatively impacted by a higher number of memberships sold online, including through digital promotions, entering the renewal rate calculation. These members renew at a slightly lower rate on average."

**The September 2024 fee increase:**
- Announced Jul 10, 2024 in Costco's June sales release; effective Sep 1, 2024; U.S. and Canada only. Gold Star, Business, Business add-on: $60 -> $65. Executive: $120 -> $130. Max 2% reward: $1,000 -> $1,250. "The fee increases will impact around 52 million memberships, a little over half of which are Executive." (proven, Costco release)
- Previous increase: June 2017 (Retail Dive https://www.retaildive.com/news/costco-raise-membership-fee/721087/ ; CFO Dive https://www.cfodive.com/news/costco-hikes-membership-fees-8-inflation-eases/721163/). probable. CFO Dive also cites D.A. Davidson's Michael Baker: historical cadence of a hike "about every 5.5 years."
- Expected effect (analyst estimates, NOT Costco guidance):
  - William Blair (Phillip Blee): "roughly $350 million in incremental sales and operating profit" over two years, "with two-thirds flowing through the model in fiscal 2025 and the remainder primarily in the first half of 2026" (Retail Dive, Jul 2024). probable.
  - D.A. Davidson (Michael Baker): $377M total revenue over 24 months, ~$189M/year, ~$0.31 EPS (CFO Dive, Jul 11, 2024). probable.
  - Costco's IR (Josh Dahmen) said the benefit would "play out over the next two fiscal years" (Retail Dive paraphrase). probable.
- Actual reported effect (Costco):
  - 10-K FY2026: the fee increase "accounted for approximately 30% and 40% of membership income growth during 2026 and 2025." proven.
  - Rough dollar translation (my arithmetic, approximate): FY25 fee growth $495M x ~40% = ~$198M; FY26 fee growth $584M x ~30% = ~$175M; about $373M over two years. That lines up with the analysts' $350M-$377M estimates. Label it "roughly" on screen, because "approximately 40%/30%" are Costco's rounded figures.
  - Quarterly commentary from CFO Millerchip (third-party transcript excerpts surfaced via search, not read in full; probable): Q1 FY25 the increase was "less than 1% of the fee growth"; Q2 FY25 "approximately 3% of fee income"; Q3 FY25 "approximately 4.6% of fee income"; Q4 FY25 "a little less than half of the membership fee income growth in the quarter" (e.g. https://www.insidermonkey.com/blog/costco-wholesale-corporation-nasdaqcost-q4-2025-earnings-call-transcript-1619860/, paywalled).
  - Q4 fee growth from Costco's own release: Q4 FY25 membership fees $1,724M vs $1,512M = +14.0% (1,724 / 1,512 - 1). proven. Q4 FY26 (Sep 24, 2026): fee income $1.849B, +7.3%; the increase "accounted for less than 1% of fee growth"; "Q4 marks the last quarter in which we will see a year-over-year benefit from the membership fee increase" (Webull transcript https://www.webull.com/news/15660435456459776). probable.

**Costco executives on what the fee means (proven, primary-quote article):**
- Then-CEO Craig Jelinek, Issaquah Chamber of Commerce luncheon, Apr 12, 2018 (425 Business, Todd Matthews, Apr 18, 2018, https://www.425business.com/news/costco-ceo-craig-jelinek-on-shareholders-costco-com-and-hot-dogs/article_5ff4b632-1f75-5e98-b9ff-6e02d676668b.html):
  - "Obviously, if you are someone like me, that's your biggest fear — you start losing memberships, you start losing traffic, and nothing good can happen."
  - "Last year, we made about $3 billion pre-tax in membership fees. That's how important that fee is to us."
  - "Everything that we do at Costco is not to figure out how much we can make; it's (about) how little we can make and still pay our bills because we want to sell more. We want to continue to lower the price and sell more units. It gets us more gross margin dollars to pay the bills."

**Inflation context for the fee (proven arithmetic, BLS CPI-U annual averages 2017 = 245.120, 2025 = 321.943):** $60 in 2017 is about $78.80 in 2025 dollars, so the $65 fee is lower in real terms than the 2017 fee.

## The hot dog combo

**Price and timeline**
- $1.50 for a hot dog plus soda since the mid-1980s. NPR (Rachel Treisman, Jun 3, 2024): "a smooth $1.50 since it first hit menus in the mid-1980s." CFO Gary Millerchip on the Sep 25, 2025 call "noted the company recently celebrated the 40th anniversary of its hot dog and soda offering" (CFO Dive, Sep 26, 2025), which dates it to 1985. Some outlets say 1984. Safe script wording: "since 1985" with "Costco celebrated its 40th anniversary in 2025" as backup, or "since the mid-1980s". probable.
- Units: 245.1M combos sold globally in FY25 (ASM 2026 deck, proven); 229M in FY24 (ASM 2025 deck, proven); "nearly 200 million" in FY23 (NPR, probable). In 2018 Jelinek said Costco sells on average 135 million hot dogs a year (425 Business, proven quote).
- 245.1M / 365 = ~671,500 combos a day on average (arithmetic). Note "globally": combo prices outside the U.S. are in local currency, so do not multiply by $1.50.
- Inflation check (BLS CPI-U, proven arithmetic): $1.50 x (321.943 / 107.6) = **$4.49 in 2025 dollars**; using Oct 2026 CPI-U (337.511) = **~$4.71**. Media figures range from ~$4.40 (NPR 2024) to ~$5 (USA Today via The Ringer); use your own CPI number.

**What changed and what did not**
- Hot dog supplier: Hebrew National was the original supplier; Costco began operating its own hot dog factories in 2008 (NPR 2024). probable. Jelinek (2018) said Costco built its own Kirkland Signature hot dog plant in Los Angeles and later a second in Chicago (425 Business). proven quote.
- Hot dog: described as a quarter-pound dog (NPR 2024). probable. Costco's food court director Bob Collins told The Ringer the Kirkland frank is about 9% larger than the original (The Ringer, Jul 2, 2026, https://www.theringer.com/2026/07/02/food/costco-hot-dog-history-explained-cheap). probable.
- Drink: 20-ounce soda (NPR refers to Costco's "20-ounce sodas"). probable.
- Soda brand: Costco switched its food-court fountain drinks from Coca-Cola to Pepsi in 2013; Fortune says that move was to protect the combo price. At the shareholder meeting in January 2025 (Jan 23), CEO Ron Vachris said: "This summer we will be converting our food-court fountain business back over to Coca-Cola" (Fortune, Jan 27, 2025, https://fortune.com/2025/01/27/costco-food-courts-coke-pepsi-swap/). Rollout from summer 2025. probable (third-party report of a live statement).
- Other food-court prices DID rise: in July 2022 the chicken bake went from $2.99 to $3.99 and standalone "20-ounce sodas, which were formerly priced at $0.59, are now selling for $0.69," while the $1.50 combo stayed (NBC Bay Area, Jul 8, 2022, https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/national-international/costco-food-court-price-increase-chicken-bake-pizza/2940582/ ; NPR 2024 also mentions the 2022 increases). probable.

**Executive promises on the price**
- Millerchip, first earnings call as CFO, May 30, 2024: "To clear up some recent media speculation, I also want to confirm the $1.50 hot dog price is safe." (NPR/OPB, Jun 2024, https://www.opb.org/article/2024/06/04/costco-hot-dogs-have-cost-1-50-since-the-1980s-here-s-why-prices-arent-changing/). probable (widely reported verbatim).
- Vachris, Instagram video, March 2026: "The hot dog price will not change as long as I'm around." (Fortune, Mar 21, 2026, updated Jul 4, 2026, https://fortune.com/article/costco-hotdog-price-always-1-50-ceo-says/ ; also Scripps News). probable.
- Former CFO Richard Galanti called it "sacrosanct" in 2022 (Fortune, same article). probable.

**The Sinegal line: original source and exact wording (proven)**
- Original source: 425 Business, "Costco CEO Craig Jelinek on Shareholders, Costco.com, and Hot Dogs," by Todd Matthews, published Apr 18, 2018, reporting Jelinek's keynote at the Issaquah Chamber of Commerce luncheon on Apr 12, 2018. URL: https://www.425business.com/news/costco-ceo-craig-jelinek-on-shareholders-costco-com-and-hot-dogs/article_5ff4b632-1f75-5e98-b9ff-6e02d676668b.html
- Exact passage (Jelinek speaking; parentheses are 425 Business's editorial insertions):
  > "I came to (Jim Sinegal) once and I said, 'Jim, we can't sell this hot dog for a buck fifty. We are losing our rear ends.' And he said, 'If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out.' That's all I really needed. By the way, if you raised (the price) to $1.75, it would not be that big of a deal. People would still buy (it). But it's the mindset that when you think of Costco, you think of the $1.50 hot dog (and soda).
  > "What we figured out we could do is build our own hot dog-manufacturing plant (in Los Angeles) and make our own Kirkland Signature hot dogs. Now we are doing so much hot dog business that we've opened up another plant in Chicago.
  > "By having the discipline to say, 'You are not going to be able to raise your price. You have to figure it out,' we took it over and started manufacturing our hot dogs. We keep it at $1.50 and make enough money to get a fair return."
- This is Jelinek's second-hand account; Sinegal's words come only through Jelinek. Say "Jelinek has recalled that Sinegal told him..." The word is "effing" in the source, not a spelled-out expletive, and the line is "raise the effing hot dog" (not "raise the price of the hot dog").

**Is the hot dog a loss leader? (thesis test)**
- In Jelinek's 2018 telling, the combo was losing money before Costco made its own hot dogs; after that, "We keep it at $1.50 and make enough money to get a fair return." So Costco's own former CEO describes it as profitable after vertical integration, not as a loss leader. proven (quote).
- Analysts call it loss-leader pricing: NPR quotes Brendan Witcher (Forrester) saying Costco is "'buying' customers' loyalty for whatever price they are unwilling to charge above $1.50." probable.
- Costco has never disclosed the combo's margin. Safest framing: "a price Costco treats as untouchable; whether it loses money today is not public, and the former CEO said it earns a fair return."

## Rotisserie chicken

- **Price:** $4.99 in the U.S.; CNN (Oct 2019, via CBS Baltimore): "Costco has sold rotisserie chickens at $4.99 since 2009." probable. Still $4.99 in 2026 according to consumer and media reporting (no Costco price list found). probable.
- **Units sold:**
  - FY25: 157.4M globally (ASM 2026 deck); Millerchip on the Sep 25, 2025 call: "over 157 million rotisserie chickens" (CFO Dive). proven.
  - FY24: 154M globally (ASM 2025 deck). proven.
  - 2018: 91M (CNN 2019, "more than double the number from a decade prior"); Jelinek in April 2018 said 87M on average per year (425 Business). probable; the two 2018-era numbers differ slightly.
  - 2014: "over 76 million" (Fortune, May 29, 2015). probable.
  - 157.4M / 365 = ~431,000 chickens a day (arithmetic).
- **Does Costco lose money on it?**
  - Then-CFO Richard Galanti, 2015 analyst call: "When others were raising their chicken prices from $4.99 to $5.99, we were willing to eat, if you will, $30 to $40 million a year in gross margin by keeping it at $4.99." (Fortune, May 29, 2015, https://fortune.com/2015/05/29/costco-chicken-prices/). probable (reported quote). This is **gross margin given up compared with charging $5.99**, not proof that each chicken is sold below cost.
  - In 2019, Jeff Lyons, Costco SVP of fresh foods, "declined to say whether Costco still loses money on the chickens," calling it a "very, very good business and very consistent growth for a long period of time" (CNN via WTOP, Oct 11, 2019, https://wtop.com/business-finance/2019/10/its-only-4-99-but-costcos-rotisserie-chicken-comes-at-a-huge-price/). probable.
  - Verdict: "Costco loses money on rotisserie chicken" is **unverified**. "Costco has been willing to give up margin to keep it at $4.99" is supported.
- **Lincoln Premium Poultry (Fremont, Nebraska):**
  - Groundbreaking announced by Nebraska's governor: "Projected to open in April 2019, the $300-million project ... create approximately 800 new jobs," processing "an expected 2 million chickens a week" (Nebraska Dept. of Economic Development, Jun 20, 2017). proven (government release) for the projection.
  - Opening: first birds processed mid-September 2019 (meatpoultry.com, via search); ribbon cutting Oct 19, 2019. At that event: "Over $450 million has been invested in the feed mill, the hatchery and the processing plant"; nearly 1,100 employees expected when fully staffed (Supermarket News/Feedstuffs, Oct 23, 2019). probable.
  - Role: NPR (Oct 22, 2018) describes Lincoln Premium Poultry as "the company started by Costco to build and manage the project"; its spokeswoman said Nebraska/Iowa barns "will supply about 40 percent of Costco's needs" (https://www.npr.org/2018/10/22/659561091/costco-builds-nebraska-supply-chain-for-its-5-rotisserie-chickens). CNN 2019: at full production about 100 million chickens a year, 40% of Costco's annual chicken needs. probable.
  - Why: NPR 2018 says the Nebraska system "will help keep the price fixed at $4.99" (plentiful corn for feed; control over bird size suited to rotisserie). probable.
- **Inflation check (BLS CPI-U, proven arithmetic):** $4.99 in 2009 (CPI 214.537) = **~$7.49 in 2025 dollars** (CPI 321.943); ~$7.85 at Oct 2026 CPI.
- **New food-court item shown in the Jan 2026 deck ("New KS Food Court items"):** a Rotisserie Chicken Chef Salad at $7.99. proven.

## Markup policy

- **Best credible source:** The New York Times, Steven Greenhouse, "How Costco Became the Anti-Wal-Mart," Jul 17, 2005 (https://www.nytimes.com/2005/07/17/business/yourmoney/how-costco-became-the-antiwalmart.html): "At Costco, one of Mr. Sinegal's cardinal rules is that no branded item can be marked up by more than 14 percent, and no private-label item by more than 15 percent. In contrast, supermarkets generally mark up merchandise by 25 percent, and department stores by 50 percent or more." The article continues that Sinegal "warned that if Costco increased markups to 16 or 18 percent, the company might slip down a dangerous slope." NYT blocked automated fetching; I verified the wording via a verbatim repost of the article (https://marprelate.blogspot.com/2005/07/how-costco-became-anti-wal-mart.html). probable.
- Earlier: Fortune (Nov 2003, "The Only Company Wal-Mart Fears," as quoted by NW Progressive Institute): "He caps Costco's markups at 14%." probable.
- **What Costco says today (proven/probable):** the 10-K does NOT state any markup cap. It says Kirkland Signature products are "offered at prices that are generally lower than national brands" and "generally earn higher margins." ASM 2026 deck: "20% value to the national brand." Q4 FY26 call: KS items offer "savings of at least 15% to 20% compared to the national brand equivalent with equal or better quality" (Webull transcript; probable).
- **Sanity check from the 10-K (proven arithmetic):** the company-wide markup over merchandise cost was 30,026 / 239,886 = **12.5%** in FY25 (12.3% in FY24, 12.5% in FY26). That is consistent with a 14-15% item cap, but it is a blended average across everything, gasoline included, after the 2% Executive reward is deducted from sales.
- **Markup vs margin (avoid a common slip):** a 14% markup on cost equals a 12.3% gross margin on price (14/114); 15% markup = 13.0% margin (15/115). Costco's reported gross margin was 11.12% in FY25.
- Script wording: "Costco has long been reported to cap markups at 14% on name brands and 15% on Kirkland Signature, according to a 2005 New York Times profile of co-founder Jim Sinegal." Do not call it an official, disclosed, current policy.

## Surprising facts (pattern interrupts)

1. **Costco keeps about 3 cents of every dollar.** FY25 net income was $3.00 per $100 of net sales, and FY25 was its "First >4% pre-tax profit year in company history" (ASM 2026 deck; 10,818 / 269,912 = 4.01%). proven.
2. **Gold bars.** Bullion sales grew +135% in FY25 (ASM 2026 deck, proven). Costco started selling gold bars in late summer 2023; they brought in about $100 million in fiscal Q1 2024; Wells Fargo analyst Edward Kelly estimated sales "may now be running at" $100-200 million a month and called it "a very low profit business at best" (CNBC, Apr 9, 2024, https://www.cnbc.com/2024/04/09/costco-selling-up-to-200-million-in-gold-bars-a-month-wells-fargo-estimates.html). The $100-200M/month figure is an analyst estimate from April 2024, not a Costco number. probable.
3. **Kirkland Signature is a ~$90 billion brand.** "FY 2025 sales: ~$90B" (ASM 2026 deck), about one-third of Costco's $269.9B net sales (90 / 269.9 = 33%). proven.
4. **The card-scanning crackdown.** In June 2023 Costco said: "We don't feel it's right that non-members receive the same benefits and pricing as our members." In August 2024 its customer-service site said: "Over the coming months, membership scanning devices will be used at the entrance door of your local warehouse." (Fortune, Aug 7, 2024, https://fortune.com/2024/08/07/costco-member-card-scanning-membership-crackdown/). Cards without a photo require photo ID. The crackdown came weeks before the Sep 1, 2024 fee increase. probable.
5. **Executive members are half the paid members but three-quarters of the sales.** 42.3M of 84.1M paid members, ~75.2% of worldwide net sales in FY26 (10-K FY2026). Since June 30, 2025 they also get exclusive shopping hours (ASM 2026 deck). proven.
6. **Wages and CEO pay.** ~$32 U.S. average hourly wage and 9% U.S. turnover after one year (ASM 2026 deck, proven). In March 2025 Costco raised its minimum wage to $20/hour, with $1 raises at the top of scale in March 2025, 2026 and 2027 (Fortune/Retail Brew, Mar 11, 2025, https://fortune.com/2025/03/11/costco-workers-make-31-an-hour-raises-for-next-two-years/; probable). CEO Ron Vachris's FY25 total compensation was $13,932,597 vs a median employee's $49,186, a 283:1 ratio; for full-time employees only, the median is $66,262 and the ratio 210:1 (DEF 14A, proven).
7. **671,000 hot dog combos and 431,000 rotisserie chickens a day**, on average, in FY25 (245.1M and 157.4M / 365, proven arithmetic from the ASM 2026 deck).

## Nuance and counterpoints

**Thesis verdict: partly true. Membership fees are about half of Costco's operating profit, a slim majority on that measure, but the stores also make money on their own. The hot dog's "loss leader" status is not supported by Costco's own statements.**

1. **"Most of the profit" is borderline.** Fees were 51.3% of operating income in FY25 and 50.6% in FY26 (52.0% in FY24). On a pre-tax income basis (which adds net interest income) they were 49.2% in FY25 (5,323 / 10,818) and 48.2% in FY26 (5,907 / 12,251), just under half. Defensible script line: "Membership fees, about 2% of Costco's revenue, equal roughly half of its operating profit."
2. **The merchandise business is profitable without fees.** FY25: gross margin $30,026M minus SG&A $24,966M = **$5,060M** (1.87% of net sales). FY26: $32,968M - $27,190M = $5,778M. Selling stuff covers all of Costco's operating costs and leaves about $5-6B. Costco does not split SG&A between "membership" and "merchandise", so this split is an analytical simplification (and the Executive 2% reward is deducted from sales, so the merchandise side already carries that membership perk).
3. **Fees and low prices are the same machine.** Members pay because prices are low, and the fee lets Costco keep prices low. The 10-K says the format "is designed to reinforce member loyalty and provide continuing fee revenue." Jelinek: losing memberships is "your biggest fear."
4. **Fee growth has slowed.** The Sep 2024 increase drove ~40% of FY25 and ~30% of FY26 fee growth. By Q4 FY26 it was "less than 1%" of fee growth and Costco said that quarter was the last with a year-over-year benefit. Q4 FY26 fee income grew 7.3% ($1,849M, per the call transcript; probable), versus 14.0% in Q4 FY25 ($1,724M vs $1,512M, Costco's FY25 release; proven). Renewal rates slipped from 92.9%/90.5% (FY24) to 92.3%/89.8% (FY25 and FY26), which Costco attributes to lower-renewing online sign-ups.
5. **"What if fees went away?"** Pure arithmetic, all else equal: FY25 operating income would fall from $10,383M to $5,060M (-51%). But all else would not be equal: without fees there would be no member base, renewal loop or traffic as we know it. Present this as a thought experiment, not a forecast.
6. **Is the hot dog a loss leader?** Former CEO Jelinek (2018): "We keep it at $1.50 and make enough money to get a fair return." It lost money before Costco made its own hot dogs. Better framing: a "price promise" or "trust anchor," protected by vertical integration (own hot dog plants) and a cheaper soda deal (Pepsi in 2013).
7. **Is the chicken a loss leader?** Costco has said it gave up $30-40M a year in gross margin (2015) rather than raise the price; in 2019 it declined to say whether it still loses money. Unproven either way. Costco built a $450M+ poultry complex partly to keep the price at $4.99.
8. **Cheap price promises are a small part of sales.** As a rough order of magnitude only (not a revenue figure, since non-U.S. combos are priced in local currency): 245.1M x $1.50 = ~$368M, about 0.14% of $269.9B net sales. Its value is psychological (trust, traffic), which no filing quantifies.
9. **Low markups do not mean low prices on everything.** The 14%/15% cap is a 2005-reported rule; the 10-K says Kirkland earns higher margins than national brands.

## Breakdown for chart (with arithmetic)

All inputs from the FY2025 10-K income statement (millions): net sales 269,912; membership fees 5,323; merchandise costs 239,886; SG&A 24,966; operating income 10,383; interest expense 154; interest income and other 589; income before taxes 10,818; taxes 2,719; net income 8,099.

**Chart A: "For every $100 rung up at the register (net sales), FY2025"**

| Line | Arithmetic | Per $100 of net sales |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of the goods | 239,886 / 269,912 x 100 | **$88.88** |
| Gross margin | 30,026 / 269,912 x 100 | $11.12 |
| Operating costs (SG&A: wages, warehouses, etc.) | 24,966 / 269,912 x 100 | **$9.25** |
| Left over from selling merchandise | 11.12 - 9.25 | **$1.87** |
| Plus membership fees (expressed per $100 of sales) | 5,323 / 269,912 x 100 | **$1.97** |
| = Operating income | 10,383 / 269,912 x 100 | $3.85 (1.87 + 1.97 = 3.84; difference is rounding) |
| Plus net interest and other income | (589 - 154) / 269,912 x 100 | $0.16 |
| = Pre-tax income | 10,818 / 269,912 x 100 | $4.01 |
| Minus income taxes | 2,719 / 269,912 x 100 | $1.01 |
| = **Net income** | 8,099 / 269,912 x 100 | **$3.00** |

Script line: "Of every $100 you spend at the register, about $89 pays for the goods, about $9 runs the business, and about $2 is left. Membership fees add about another $2. After tax, Costco keeps about $3."

Same table for FY2026 (10-K FY2026: net sales 297,247; merchandise costs 264,279; SG&A 27,190; fees 5,907; operating income 11,685; pre-tax 12,251; tax 3,025; net income 9,226): goods $88.91; gross margin $11.09; SG&A $9.15; left from merchandise $1.94; fees $1.99; operating income $3.93; pre-tax $4.12; tax $1.02; net income $3.10.

Alternative, if you want "$100 of everything members pay Costco" including fees (FY2025 total revenue 275,235): merchandise costs $87.16; SG&A $9.07; operating income $3.77; net income $2.94; membership fees are $1.93 of every $100.

**Chart B: "Membership fees vs operating income"**

| Fiscal year | Membership fees | Operating income | Merchandise contribution (op. income - fees) | Fees as % of op. income |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FY2024 | $4,828M | $9,285M | $4,457M | 4,828 / 9,285 = **52.0%** |

## Do NOT claim (myths or numbers you could not verify)

- **"Costco makes all/nearly all of its profit from membership fees"** or "fees are 65-70%+ of profit." Fees are ~51% of operating income (FY25). Figures like "64% of profits" come from dividing pre-tax fee revenue by after-tax net income (FY26: 5,907 / 9,226 = 64.0%; FY25: 65.7%), which mixes pre-tax and after-tax numbers.
- **"Costco sells merchandise at break-even/at a loss."** The merchandise side earned ~$5.06B after all SG&A in FY25.
- **"Costco loses money on every hot dog."** No current evidence; Jelinek (2018) said they make "a fair return" after building their own plants.
- **"Costco loses $X per rotisserie chicken" / "loses $30-40 million a year on chicken."** The $30-40M (2015) was gross margin given up versus pricing at $5.99, not a confirmed loss; Costco declined in 2019 to say whether it loses money.
- **"$350 million from the fee hike was Costco's forecast."** It was William Blair's estimate (and $377M was D.A. Davidson's, not Bloomberg Intelligence's as some summaries say). Costco only reported ~40%/~30% of fee growth.
- **Exact Sinegal quote variants** such as "If you raise the price of the f***ing hot dog, I will kill you." Use the 425 Business wording: "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out." It is Jelinek's recollection, not a recorded Sinegal statement.
- **The 14%/15% markup cap as an official, current, disclosed policy.** It is reported (NYT 2005, Fortune 2003) as Sinegal's rule; it is not in the 10-K.
- **Hot dog "revenue" = 245.1M x $1.50 = $368M.** The 245.1M is global; non-U.S. combos are priced in local currency.
- **Kirkland growth from "$56B to $90B."** A $56B figure reported by Fortune (Jun 2024) for an earlier year may not be measured the same way as the deck's ~$90B; don't compute growth between them.
- **The rotisserie chicken "should cost $11+ with inflation"** (Tasting Table). CPI-U gives ~$7.49 (2025) from $4.99 in 2009.
- **The chicken briefly went to $5.99 in the 2008 recession.** Only a food-blog source (Tasting Table); unverified.
- **"Chickens/milk are at the back of the store to make you walk past everything."** A common claim; no Costco source found.
- **Mixing renewal-rate dates.** The Jan 2025 deck shows 92.8%/90.4% (as of Q1 FY25); the FY24 year-end 10-K figures are 92.9%/90.5%. Cite one period consistently.
- **"Gold sales of $200M a month" as a Costco number.** It was a Wells Fargo estimate (Apr 2024), upper end of a $100-200M range.
- **The exact first year of the combo (1984 vs 1985)** as hard fact. Say "since 1985, per Costco's 40th-anniversary framing" or "since the mid-1980s."
- **That the 2013 Pepsi switch was officially explained by Costco as protecting the combo price.** That's Fortune's characterization; I found no Costco statement.
- **"Costco has never raised any food-court price."** It raised the chicken bake and standalone soda in 2022.
- **Comparisons with Sam's Club or BJ's economics.** Not researched here.
- **Lincoln Premium Poultry ownership details** (e.g. "wholly owned by Costco"). Sources say it was "started by Costco" and operates "on Costco's behalf"; exact ownership was not confirmed.
- **"Membership scanners boosted sign-ups by X%."** Executives said there was a slight lift; no figure was given.
