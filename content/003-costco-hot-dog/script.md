## VOICE-READY narration (paste into the voice tool, one ¶ at a time)

**[HOOK]**

¶1 This hot dog has cost one dollar fifty since nineteen eighty-five. [pause] Adjusted for inflation, it should cost about four dollars and fifty cents. Costco sold two hundred forty-five million of these combos in fiscal twenty twenty-five. That's about six hundred seventy thousand a day. And most people assume Costco loses money on every single one.

¶2 It doesn't. At least, that's what Costco's own former CEO says. The real trick isn't in the food court. It's a sixty-five dollar card in your wallet. And by the end of this video, you'll see why Costco keeps only about three cents of every dollar you spend there — and why that's exactly the plan.

**[CHAPTER 1 — A store that tries not to make money]**

¶3 Most companies ask one question: how much can we make? In a twenty eighteen speech, Costco's then-CEO Craig Jelinek described the opposite. Quote: "Everything that we do at Costco is not to figure out how much we can make; it's about how little we can make and still pay our bills." [pause] That sounds like a slogan. The annual report says it isn't.

¶4 In fiscal twenty twenty-five, for every one hundred dollars rung up at a Costco register, eighty-eight dollars and eighty-eight cents went straight back out to pay for the goods themselves. That's the first line on our receipt.

¶5 For context: a two thousand five New York Times profile reported that co-founder Jim Sinegal capped markups at fourteen percent on name brands, and fifteen percent on Costco's own Kirkland Signature — at a time when supermarkets typically marked up around twenty-five percent, and department stores fifty percent or more. That cap was never written into Costco's filings. But the math still fits. Across everything Costco sold in fiscal twenty twenty-five, the average markup over cost works out to about twelve and a half percent.

¶6 So if the margins are that thin, how do you sell a hot dog and a soda for a dollar fifty — for forty years?

**[CHAPTER 2 — Clue one: the hot dog]**

¶7 In that same speech, Jelinek told a story. Years earlier, he had gone to Sinegal and said: "Jim, we can't sell this hot dog for a buck fifty. We are losing our rear ends." [pause] Sinegal's answer, as Jelinek remembers it: "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out."

¶8 So they figured it out. Costco stopped buying its hot dogs and started making them — first in its own plant in Los Angeles, then in a second one in Chicago. And here's the line almost nobody quotes. Jelinek again: "We keep it at a dollar fifty and make enough money to get a fair return."

¶9 Read that again. According to the man who ran the company, the hot dog stopped losing money once Costco controlled the factory. It isn't charity. It's engineering.

¶10 And the price isn't untouchable because nothing else changes. In twenty thirteen, Costco switched its fountain drinks from Coca-Cola to Pepsi — a move Fortune linked to protecting the combo price. In January twenty twenty-five, CEO Ron Vachris announced a switch back to Coca-Cola. And in twenty twenty-two, the food court raised the chicken bake from two ninety-nine to three ninety-nine, and a soda on its own from fifty-nine cents to sixty-nine. The combo didn't move.

¶11 Quick question before we go on. [pause] What's the one thing you buy at Costco every single time? Tell us in the comments. We read them.

**[CHAPTER 3 — Clue two: the chicken]**

¶12 Then there's the rotisserie chicken: four ninety-nine in the US, a price Costco has reportedly held since two thousand nine. Pause the video and guess how many Costco sold in fiscal twenty twenty-five. [pause] One hundred fifty-seven point four million. That's roughly four hundred thirty thousand chickens a day.

¶13 Adjusted for inflation, a four ninety-nine chicken from two thousand nine would cost about seven fifty in twenty twenty-five dollars. So is this one a loss? In twenty fifteen, Costco's then-CFO Richard Galanti said that when others raised chicken prices to five ninety-nine, Costco was, quote, "willing to eat, if you will, thirty to forty million dollars a year in gross margin" to stay at four ninety-nine.

¶14 Notice the wording. That's margin Costco chose not to collect — not proof that it sells each chicken below cost. In twenty nineteen, a Costco executive declined to say whether it still loses money on them. What we do know: Costco started a poultry company in Fremont, Nebraska — a feed mill, a hatchery and a processing plant — with more than four hundred fifty million dollars invested by twenty nineteen, reportedly to supply about forty percent of its chickens. You don't build a supply chain like that for a product you plan to lose money on forever.

¶15 So the hot dog earns a fair return. The chicken might be close to break-even. Which leaves the real question: where does Costco's profit actually come from?

**[CHAPTER 4 — The twist: the card]**

¶16 Back to our receipt. For every one hundred dollars at the register: eighty-eight eighty-eight for the goods. Nine dollars and twenty-five cents for everything it takes to run the business — wages, warehouses, trucks, electricity. That leaves one dollar and eighty-seven cents from actually selling you things.

¶17 Now add one more line — one that never shows up at the checkout. Membership fees. In fiscal twenty twenty-five, Costco collected five point three billion dollars in membership fees. That's less than two percent of its revenue. But Costco's entire operating profit that year was ten point four billion. [pause] So that card — under two percent of the money coming in — equals about half of Costco's operating profit.

¶18 About half. Not "almost all." You'll see claims online that membership is sixty-five or seventy percent of Costco's profit. Those numbers divide before-tax fees by after-tax profit. Done properly, it's fifty-one percent in fiscal twenty twenty-five, and fifty point six percent in fiscal twenty twenty-six. The stores make money too. But the card is the single biggest line on this receipt.

¶19 And people keep paying it. At the end of fiscal twenty twenty-six, Costco had eighty-four point one million paid members, and ninety-two point three percent of members in the US and Canada renewed. In September twenty twenty-four, Costco raised the fee — from sixty to sixty-five dollars, and from one hundred twenty to one hundred thirty for Executive members. That increase alone drove about forty percent of membership-fee growth in fiscal twenty twenty-five. A few weeks before it took effect, Costco announced it would start scanning cards at the door.

¶20 One more number. Executive members — the ones paying one hundred thirty dollars — were about half of all paid members in fiscal twenty twenty-six. But they accounted for roughly seventy-five percent of Costco's sales.

**[CHAPTER 5 — Why the loop works]**

¶22 Here's why this works. The fee lets Costco keep prices thin. Thin prices make the card feel worth it. Members renew. The fee comes back. Jelinek called losing memberships "your biggest fear" — because when memberships go, traffic goes, and, in his words, "nothing good can happen."

¶23 The hot dog is the advertisement for that loop. It's a dollar-fifty promise that says: the prices in here are honest. In twenty twenty-four, Costco's CFO told investors, quote, "the one fifty hot dog price is safe." In twenty twenty-six, CEO Ron Vachris said it "will not change as long as I'm around." [pause] You don't make promises like that about a snack. You make them about a brand.

¶24 Try a thought experiment. Take the fees out of fiscal twenty twenty-five, change nothing else, and operating profit falls from about ten point four billion dollars to about five point one. But "change nothing else" is impossible. Without the card, there's no loyalty loop, no renewal rate, and no reason to drive past three other stores to get there.

¶25 And that loop now runs on four continents. At the end of fiscal twenty twenty-six, Costco operated nine hundred thirty-nine warehouses: six hundred forty-seven in the US and Puerto Rico, one hundred fifteen in Canada, twenty-nine in the UK, and fifteen in Australia — plus more across Asia and Europe.

**[CHAPTER 6 — The verdict]**

¶26 So, the verdict. After paying for the goods, the staff, the buildings and the taxes, Costco kept about three dollars of every hundred you spent in fiscal twenty twenty-five. About half of its operating profit came from a card. And the hot dog that hasn't changed price since nineteen eighty-five? According to the man who ran Costco, it isn't a loss leader. It's a contract.

¶27 One bit of math, if you're choosing a membership — information, not advice. Executive costs sixty-five dollars more than Gold Star, and pays a two percent reward on qualifying purchases. Sixty-five divided by two percent is three thousand two hundred fifty. Below that much qualifying spending in a year, the reward doesn't cover the upgrade.

¶28 Costco isn't the only company where the real product is the card in your wallet. Airlines run their own version of this trick — with credit cards. That breakdown is right here.
