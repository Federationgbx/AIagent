## Suspect 1 — Distance ("the tyranny of distance")
- **Border cost of distance:** international freight = about **3–4 cents per dollar** of imported goods before COVID (ABS 2023). It doubled to ~7% in early 2022 (ABS; RBA 2025 repeats "doubled to around 7 per cent"). Road vehicles stayed at 3–5% even in the pandemic (roll-on/roll-off ships); furniture peaked at 19% (Feb 2022). — proven
- **How much of the basket can be shipped at all:** about **a quarter** of the CPI basket (RBA 2025). The rest is services, rent, utilities, local fresh food, and government-set items. — proven
- **Back-of-envelope (Pricewhy estimate, label it as ours):** 25% of the basket × 4% freight ≈ **1 cent per dollar** of the whole basket. (Caveat: the RBA's quarter leaves out volatile items such as fuel, and our sources don't quantify fuel's sea-freight share — so this is an approximation. The two big simplifications — all shippable goods treated as imported, border value used instead of shop price — both push it up, so the script may say "if anything, on the high side" but not "an upper bound".) This is generous (it treats every shippable item as imported and ignores that the border value is below the shop price) → likely on the high side in normal times; ~1.75 cents at the 2022 peak (25% × 7%).
- **The goods that come furthest are cheap:** clothing & footwear 69.5 (US = 100), 45th of 48; vehicles 78.6, 44th of 48 (OECD 2024). Australia stopped mass-producing cars in October 2017 (Toyota Altona closed 3 Oct, Holden Elizabeth 20 Oct 2017 — GoAuto, 20 Oct 2017, https://www.goauto.com.au/news/holden/holden-finally-ends-production-after-69-years/2017-10-20/66679.html) — so new cars are shipped in, yet cost less than in the US. — proven (OECD) / probable (closure dates)
- **Where distance DOES matter:** inside Australia. ACCC: remote grocery prices are higher "primarily due to higher costs to serve these areas"; country petrol freight is 1.5–4 c/L more than city delivery (AIP fact sheet, Sep 2026 — industry body). Also during the 2021–22 shipping shock, RBA estimates global shipping costs added around **2 percentage points** to trimmed-mean inflation at the 2022 peak (RBA Jul 2025; note RBA's own caveat: much smaller and not statistically significant outside the pandemic). — proven
- **The alibi from 2013:** digital goods with **no freight at all** cost much more in Australia: the same iTunes song US$1.29 vs A$2.19 with the currencies near parity; downloaded (Steam) games showed even larger gaps. The committee blamed "regional pricing strategies" — the "Australia tax" — not distance. — proven, but old (2013): always say the year. NB the category averages (games +84%, music +52%) mix physical and digital products → don't call them "downloads".

## Suspect 2 — The supermarkets
- ACCC (FY2023): Woolworths 38%, Coles 29% → two-thirds; ALDI 9% (took "more than 20 years" since entering in 2001); Metcash-supplied independents 7%. "In economic terms, this is an oligopolistic market structure in which Coles and Woolworths have limited incentive to compete vigorously with each other on price." — proven (quote)
- Profitability "among the most profitable supermarket businesses globally"; product and EBIT margins up over 5 years. — proven
- **Fair counterpoints (same report):** most price rises came from higher costs; grocery inflation was lower than in many OECD countries; Coles argued much of its EBIT-margin rise reflects a 2019–20 accounting change for leases (AASB-16), and that its supermarkets' margin "is unlikely to have materially changed" since 2017–18. — proven (as Coles' submission)
- OECD 2024 food price level: Australia 101 vs US 100 — groceries overall are about US level, cheaper than Canada (108), dearer than the UK (85.1). Dairy & eggs 122 stands out. — proven
- Verdict for script: **contributor, not mastermind.**

## Suspect 3 — Taxes on purpose
- Tobacco 312 (US = 100), 2nd of 48; alcohol 152, 4th of 48 (OECD 2024). — proven
- WHO data via Cancer Council Victoria's *Tobacco in Australia* (updated June 2026): total tax share of the leading cigarette brand **77%** (68% excise + ~9% GST). — probable
- GST is 10% and is included in shelf prices (basic food is GST-free — not used in script).
- These are deliberate public-health / tax choices, and they don't explain the everyday basket.

## Suspect 4 — Wages (and the flip side)
- Minimum wage A$26.44/h from 1 Jul 2026 (probable). OECD 2025, USD at exchange rates: AU $15.81 ≈ UK $15.83, Canada $11.85, US federal $7.25. — proven
- Services that are mostly labour cost more: restaurants & accommodation 115 (5th of 48). — proven
- RBA Aug 2026: labour costs are the largest input into household consumption prices; since late 2022 labour costs and dwelling rents made large, persistent contributions; import price pressures eased from 2023. — proven
- **Counterpoint:** average wage (USD PPP, 2025): AU $72,018 > Canada $67,901 > UK $66,299, but < US $86,977. So: higher pay than Britain and Canada, lower than America. — proven

## The twist — the time machine (exchange rate)
- OECD household price level vs US: 2005 112 · 2008 128 · 2011 158 · **2012 160** · 2013 147 · 2015 116 · 2019 109 · 2022 98 · 2023 93 · 2024 94 · 2025 94 · Sep 2026 103 (monthly estimate). — proven (arithmetic: PPP ÷ exchange rate, OECD Table 4)
- Australia's PPP for household consumption barely moved (1.546 in 2012 → 1.465 in 2025, −5.2%). The exchange rate moved from 0.9658 to 1.552 AUD per USD (+60.7%). So the 2012 "60% dearer than America" was mostly the currency, not distance — the distance didn't change. — proven (arithmetic)
- Why the dollar was so strong: the mining/terms-of-trade boom (RBA explainer, quotes above). — proven
- Same pattern for Canada (130 in 2011 → 90 in 2025) and the UK (152 in 2007 → 92 in 2025): price levels at market exchange rates swing with currencies. — proven

## Surprising facts (pattern interrupts)
1. A Big Mac costs **less** in Australia than in the US in July 2026 ($5.95 vs $6.22) — and much less than in Britain ($7.40).
2. In 2012 the same basket cost **60% more** in Australia than in the US; in 2025, **6% less**. Same distance.
3. Clothes are among the **cheapest** of 48 countries (45th) — even though almost all of them are shipped in.
4. New cars: no local mass production since 2017, yet vehicles were **21% cheaper** than the US level (78.6) in 2024.
5. In 2013 the same iTunes song cost US$1.29 in the US and A$2.19 in Australia, with the two dollars near parity — no shipping at all.
6. Tobacco costs **about three times** the US level; only New Zealand is higher of 48 countries.
7. Two supermarket chains have **two-thirds** of supermarket grocery sales.
8. Sydney: 14 years of a typical household's gross income for a typical house — only Hong Kong is worse.
9. Minimum-wage minutes to buy a Big Mac (July 2026): Australia ≈ **19 min** (8.50 ÷ 26.44 × 60 = 19.3) · UK ≈ **26 min** (5.49 ÷ 12.71 × 60 = 25.9; UK National Living Wage £12.71 from 1 Apr 2026, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/national-living-wage-increases-to-1271-per-hour) · US ≈ **51 min at the federal minimum** (6.22 ÷ 7.25 × 60 = 51.5; many states are higher). Canada: no single minimum (provincial) → left out. — proven arithmetic / probable AU wage

## Nuance and counterpoints
- "Expensive" depends on the yardstick: at market exchange rates Australia is a little above the US today (103) and well above the UK and Canada; in 2024 it was below the US (94.2). It has never been "the most expensive country in the world" in this data (Switzerland 121, Iceland 113 in 2024).
- The feeling of expensiveness is real and recent: grocery prices rose faster than wages from late 2021 to Dec 2024 (+17% vs +11%), and housing affordability hit records in 2025.
- Wages are higher than in the UK and Canada; the minimum wage is about double the US federal minimum — that is part of why labour-heavy services cost more, and part of why Australians can pay it.
- Supermarkets: the ACCC found an oligopoly and rising margins, **and** that most price rises came from costs. Both halves go in the script.
- Distance is not zero: remote towns, the 2022 shipping shock, and furniture/appliances at peak freight show it. It's just small for the typical city basket.
- Exchange-rate based comparisons swing year to year; always say the year.

**Thesis verdict:** **mostly false** — distance adds roughly a cent or two per dollar of the typical basket (freight 3–4% of import value on about a quarter of the basket) and the most-shipped goods (clothes, cars) are among the cheapest in the OECD comparison; what makes Australia feel expensive is what's made locally — housing/land and labour-heavy services — plus taxes chosen on purpose, a concentrated grocery market, and a currency that made Australia look 60% dearer than the US in 2012.

## Do NOT claim
- "Australia is the most expensive country in the world." False in OECD data: 7th of 48 in 2024 (Switzerland, Iceland, US, Denmark, Israel, Ireland higher).
- "Distance makes everything expensive." Freight ≈ 3–4% of import value; the most-imported goods (clothes, cars) are cheap.
- "Coles and Woolworths are price gouging." The ACCC did not use that finding; it said most rises came from costs and *some* added to profits. Do not mention the ACCC's separate court cases (allegations, outcome not checked).
- "Australians pay 50–100% more for everything." That was IT products in the 2013 inquiry.
- "Australians earn more than Americans." False on average wages (US $86,977 vs AU $72,018, PPP 2025); true only for the minimum wage vs the US *federal* minimum.
- Any single cause of high house prices (negative gearing, immigration, zoning…) — contested and political; not in this video.
- "Petrol is among the cheapest in the OECD" — industry-body claim (AIP, March quarter 2026) during a year when fuel rose 23.6%; leave out.
- "Americans pay more for a Big Mac at the till" — the US figure may exclude sales tax; say "in The Economist's data".
- Old numbers without the year (2013 Australia tax, 2012 price level).
