# Script: Why Is Australia So Expensive?
Video: 005 · Structure: **Detective** (crime scene → four suspects → clues → twist → verdict) · Hook type: 1 (shock number) + 3 (false accusation: "it's distance") · Target length: ~11 min
Narration words: **1,671** (≈ 10.8 min at 155 wpm; Kokoro read 003 at ≈ 160 wpm incl. pauses → ≈ 10.4 min). Scene times below are estimates; real render: narration 10:45, final video 10:57 (chapters in build/chapters.txt).
Twist (from research verdict): distance is **not** the culprit — freight adds roughly a cent per dollar of the basket and the most-shipped goods (clothes, cars) are among the cheapest of 48 countries. The "60% dearer than America" reputation was a 2012 currency peak; what is truly extreme today is the one thing you can't ship: a home (Sydney = 14 × income).

## VOICE-READY narration (paste into the voice tool, one ¶ at a time)

**[HOOK]**

¶1 A typical house in Sydney costs fourteen times what a typical household earns in a year. Los Angeles: ten point seven. London: eight point five. In the twenty twenty-six Demographia ranking of ninety-five cities, only Hong Kong is worse. [pause] And yet, a Big Mac costs less in Australia than in America.

¶2 Ask anyone why Australia is so expensive, and you'll hear one word: distance. Everything has to cross an ocean. That's our first suspect. We put it on trial with data from the OECD, the Reserve Bank and the competition regulator. It has an alibi. By the end, you'll know who really did it. And it didn't arrive on a ship.

**[CHAPTER 1 — The crime scene]**

¶3 First: is there even a crime? The OECD prices the same basket of household spending in dozens of countries, at market exchange rates. Its estimate for September twenty twenty-six: if that basket costs one hundred dollars in the United States, it costs about one hundred and three in Australia. Ninety-two in the UK. Eighty-seven in Canada.

¶4 So Australia is a little dearer than America, about eleven percent dearer than Britain, and seventeen percent dearer than Canada. Expensive? Yes. The most expensive place on earth? No. In the OECD's full results for twenty twenty-four, Australia ranked seventh of forty-eight countries, behind Switzerland, Iceland, the United States, Denmark, Israel and Ireland.

¶5 Now the burger. In July twenty twenty-six, according to The Economist's data, a Big Mac cost eight dollars fifty in Australia. That's five ninety-five in US dollars. In the US, six twenty-two. In Canada, five eighty-one. In Britain, seven forty. On this board, Australia is the second cheapest.

¶6 So why does it feel so expensive? Two lines on the receipt explain the feeling. From late twenty twenty-one to the end of twenty twenty-four, grocery prices rose about seventeen percent, while wages rose about eleven. And homes: in September twenty twenty-five, the median Australian home cost eight point two years of median household income. A record. Saving a twenty percent deposit took eleven years. Also a record.

¶7 That's the crime scene. Now, the suspects.

**[CHAPTER 2 — Suspect one: distance]**

¶8 Distance is the obvious one. Australia is a long way from the factories that make most of what it buys. So how much does crossing the ocean actually add? The Australian Bureau of Statistics measured it. Before the pandemic, international freight cost about three to four cents for every dollar of goods Australia imported. During the shipping crisis, in early twenty twenty-two, it hit seven cents.

¶9 Next question: how much of what you buy even comes by ship? The Reserve Bank looked at the consumer price basket and found that the goods that can come by ship, leaving out volatile items like fuel, are about a quarter of it. Most of the rest is rent, services, power, and prices set by governments.

¶10 Pause and guess. If a quarter of your basket carries a four percent shipping cost, how much of every dollar is distance? [pause] About one cent. That's our own rough math, and it's generous: it treats everything shippable as imported, and it ignores that shop prices are higher than prices at the border. If anything, it's on the high side.

¶11 And here's the clue that breaks the alibi open. If distance made things expensive, the things that travel farthest would be the priciest. They aren't. In the OECD's twenty twenty-four data, clothing and shoes in Australia cost about thirty percent less than in the US. Forty-fifth of forty-eight countries. New cars cost about twenty percent less. And Australia hasn't mass-produced a car since October twenty seventeen, when Holden closed the country's last car factory. Nearly every new car arrives on a ship.

¶12 Distance does matter in one place: inside Australia. The competition regulator found that grocery prices in remote areas are higher, sometimes significantly, mainly because of the cost of serving them. But for a city shopping basket, suspect one walks free.

¶13 One more exhibit. In twenty thirteen, a parliamentary committee looked at prices for things that never touch a ship: downloads. The same song on iTunes most often cost one dollar twenty-nine in the US store and two dollars nineteen in the Australian one, when the two dollars were worth about the same. Downloaded games showed even bigger gaps. Zero freight. The committee concluded that in many cases, the gaps could not be explained by the cost of doing business in Australia. People had a name for it: the Australia tax.

¶14 Quick question before suspect two. What's the one thing you think is most overpriced where you live? Tell us in the comments. We read them.

**[CHAPTER 3 — Suspect two: the supermarkets]**

¶15 Suspect two: the supermarkets. Australia's competition regulator, the ACCC, spent a year on this. Its final report, released in March twenty twenty-five, estimates that Woolworths has about thirty-eight percent of supermarket grocery sales, and Coles twenty-nine. Together, two thirds. ALDI has nine. In the regulator's words, it's an oligopolistic market, where Coles and Woolworths have limited incentive to compete vigorously with each other on price.

¶16 The report also found that ALDI, Coles and Woolworths appear to be among the most profitable supermarket businesses in the world, and that their average product margins went up over five years.

¶17 Case closed? Not so fast. The same report says most of the grocery price rises came from higher costs across the economy, with at least some turning into extra profit. It found grocery inflation in Australia was lower than in many OECD countries. And Coles argued that most of the rise in its profit margin came from an accounting change for leases.

¶18 The OECD's price data points to the same middle ground. In twenty twenty-four, food in Australia cost about the same as in the US: one percent more. Cheaper than Canada. Dearer than Britain. The exception was dairy and eggs, at twenty-two percent above the US. Verdict on suspect two: a contributor, not the mastermind.

**[CHAPTER 4 — Suspects three and four: taxes and wages]**

¶19 Suspect three hides in plain sight. In twenty twenty-four, tobacco in Australia cost about three times the US level, the second highest of forty-eight countries, after New Zealand. Alcohol cost about fifty percent more. For the best-selling cigarette brand, World Health Organization data put the tax share at about seventy-seven percent of the price. That's a choice made by government, not a cost of distance. It makes a few things very expensive, but it doesn't explain your groceries or your rent.

¶20 Suspect four: wages. From July twenty twenty-six, Australia's minimum wage is twenty-six dollars forty-four an hour. In the OECD's comparable figures for twenty twenty-five, Australia's minimum was about fifteen eighty in US dollars. Roughly the same as Britain, and more than double America's federal minimum of seven twenty-five.

¶21 So anything made mostly of labour costs more. In twenty twenty-four, restaurants and hotels in Australia were fifteen percent above US prices, the fifth highest of forty-eight countries. The other side of that coin: on average, Australians earn more than Britons and Canadians, about seventy-two thousand US dollars a year in purchasing-power terms, against sixty-eight thousand in Canada and sixty-six in the UK. Americans still earn more, about eighty-seven thousand.

**[CHAPTER 5 — The twist: a time machine]**

¶22 Now the twist. Let's go back to twenty twelve. Using the OECD's numbers, the same basket of household spending cost about sixty percent more in Australia than in the US. Sixty percent. [pause] By twenty twenty-five, it cost about six percent less.

¶23 The distance didn't change. Australia didn't move. Prices in Australia and America rose at about the same pace. What moved was the currency. In twenty twelve, one Australian dollar bought slightly more than one US dollar. In twenty twenty-five, it bought about sixty-four cents. The Reserve Bank links that strong dollar to the resources boom, when Australia's terms of trade were around seventy-five percent above the average of the previous century.

¶24 Many of the famous comparisons, including that twenty thirteen Australia tax inquiry, come from exactly this period. So if someone tells you Australia is twice as expensive, check the year. But that leaves the real question: the dollar is much weaker now, so why does it still hurt?

¶25 The Reserve Bank broke Australian consumer prices into their inputs: labour, imports, business profits and rents. Labour is the biggest share. And since late twenty twenty-two, the persistent push has come from labour costs and housing rents, while pressure from import prices eased from twenty twenty-three. Not the ship. The things made right there.

¶26 This is what Pricewhy does. Every week, we take one price apart, line by line, with the sources on screen. If you want the next one, subscribe.

**[CHAPTER 6 — The verdict]**

¶27 So, the one thing you can't put on a ship: the land under a home. In the five years to September twenty twenty-five, Australian household incomes rose twenty percent. Home values rose fifty-three and a half. Rents rose fifty-three. Rent now takes a third of a typical household's income, the highest on record.

¶28 And that's the category where Australia really is extreme by world standards. In the twenty twenty-six Demographia ranking, four of the ten least affordable housing markets were Australian: Sydney, Adelaide, Brisbane and Melbourne. Why homes got this expensive is its own fight: supply, interest rates, migration, tax. It deserves its own episode.

¶29 Here's the verdict. Distance: a small accomplice. About a cent in the dollar in a city, more in remote towns. The supermarkets: a contributor. Taxes on tobacco: guilty, and on purpose. Wages: the price of being paid more. And the reputation for sixty percent markups: a currency that has since fallen.

¶30 Put it on the Price Board. The everyday basket: about American prices, above Britain and Canada. The minimum wage: like Britain's, double America's federal one. The average wage: above Britain and Canada, below the US. And a big-city house: fourteen years of income in Sydney, against eight and a half in London. That is the line that doesn't fit.

¶31 That's why a Sydney house costs fourteen years of income while a Big Mac costs less than in America. Speaking of burgers: most people think McDonald's is a burger company. Next time on Pricewhy, we read its annual report and find out.
