## VOICE-READY narration (paste into the voice tool, one ¶ at a time)

**[HOOK]**

¶1 This is an airline credit card. Every time someone taps one, for a coffee, for gas, for groceries, an airline gets paid. [pause] In twenty twenty-five, American Express paid Delta Air Lines eight point two billion dollars. Delta's entire pre-tax profit that year, adjusted, was about five billion.

¶2 And it isn't just Delta. American Airlines took in six point two billion dollars from its credit card and other partners in twenty twenty-five, and finished the year with one hundred eleven million dollars of net income. So where do airlines really make their money: in the air, or in your wallet? [pause] To find out, we're going to follow one dollar, from a card swipe all the way to a seat on a plane.

**[STOP 1 — The swipe]**

¶3 So you tap a Delta card at a coffee shop. Where does your dollar go first? [pause] Not to Delta. The coffee shop pays a swipe fee on every card payment, and on an American Express card, that fee goes to Amex. Add the annual fees and interest that cardholders pay, and that's the bank's side of the deal.

¶4 And for Amex, this is not a side business. Pause the video and guess: what share of everything charged on American Express cards worldwide goes on Delta cards? [pause] About thirteen percent. Roughly one dollar in every eight. Delta cards are also about twenty-one percent of all the money Amex has lent to cardholders. Those are Amex's own figures, from its twenty twenty-five annual report.

¶5 That works out to roughly two hundred seventeen billion dollars charged on Delta cards in twenty twenty-five. More than three times Delta's own revenue. So here's the first line on our receipt: the store pays a swipe fee, and it goes to the bank.

¶6 The bank has its fee. So why would it send any money to an airline?

**[STOP 2 — The bank buys miles]**

¶7 Because you didn't just buy a coffee. You earned miles, and somebody has to pay for them. Delta's annual report says it plainly: "We sell miles to American Express which are then provided to their customers." Amex pays, typically every month, based on how many miles it buys. [pause] Your coffee just became a sale for an airline you didn't even fly.

¶8 How much per mile? That's confidential. But Delta's accounting shows what's in the box. Each payment is split across a bundle: award travel, waived bag fees, lounge access, priority boarding, and, quote, "the use of our brand." The name on the card is part of what's for sale.

¶9 United has the same kind of contract with Chase. And these deals are long. Delta's current agreement with American Express runs through the end of twenty twenty-nine.

¶10 That's line two on our receipt: the bank buys miles, and the airline gets paid. [pause] Quick question before we follow the money inside Delta. What's the most miles you've ever earned without setting foot on a plane? Tell us in the comments. We read them.

**[STOP 3 — Inside the airline's books]**

¶11 So the money lands at Delta. What happens to it there? [pause] It splits. The part that pays for the brand and the marketing counts as revenue right away. In twenty twenty-five, that line was three point three six billion dollars.

¶12 The part that pays for your future seat can't count yet, because Delta hasn't flown you anywhere. It waits on the books as a liability: a trip Delta still owes. At the end of twenty twenty-five, Delta was holding nine point three billion dollars of those promises.

¶13 When you finally redeem, that money becomes revenue: four point two four billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. About twelve percent of the passenger miles Delta flew that year were award travel, some thirty-five million award tickets.

¶14 And the miles that never get used? You'll see confident numbers for that online. Delta doesn't publish one. Neither do United or American. [pause] So we won't either.

¶15 Now zoom out from one swipe to a whole year. In twenty fourteen, American Express paid Delta about two billion dollars. In twenty twenty-four, seven point four billion. In twenty twenty-five, eight point two billion, up eleven percent in a single year. Delta expects ten billion "over the next few years." That's the total at the bottom of our receipt.

**[STOP 4 — The check vs the profit]**

¶16 So is eight point two billion dollars a lot, for an airline? [pause] Put it next to the profit. Delta's adjusted pre-tax profit for twenty twenty-five was four point nine eight billion. The check from one bank was bigger than the profit of the entire airline.

¶17 To be fair, Delta's unadjusted pre-tax profit was higher, six point two billion. But that includes a one point two billion dollar gain on investments, which has nothing to do with flying. Either way, the Amex cash is bigger. Twenty twenty-four looked the same: seven point four billion from Amex, five point two billion of adjusted pre-tax profit.

¶18 And in twenty twenty-six, the gap gets wider. Delta expects its fuel bill to jump sixty percent, about six billion dollars. It now expects roughly four and a half billion of pre-tax profit, and "more than nine billion" from American Express. About two dollars from the bank for every dollar of profit.

¶19 Why so little profit? Because flying is a thin-margin business. IATA, the global airline trade group, puts the industry's net margin at three point four percent in twenty twenty-four, and four point two in twenty twenty-five. For twenty twenty-six, with fuel soaring, it forecasts two percent: about four dollars and fifty cents of profit per passenger. Its director general said that "won't even buy you a hot dog at most of the FIFA World Cup venues."

¶20 American shows what thin really looks like. Fifty-four point six billion dollars of revenue in twenty twenty-five, and one hundred eleven million of net income. About twenty cents of profit for every hundred dollars, in a year when its card partners paid it six point two billion.

¶21 So airlines are just banks with wings, right? [pause] Not so fast. Here's the part that doesn't fit the easy story: cash is not profit. Part of that money gets spent later, on the award seats, the lounges and the waived bag fees the bank paid for. Those cost real money to provide.

¶22 American actually put a number on it. According to a December twenty twenty-four filing, as its card cash approaches ten billion dollars a year, American expects its pre-tax income to rise by about one point five billion, compared to twenty twenty-four. By our math, that's roughly thirty-four to thirty-eight cents of extra profit for every extra dollar from the bank. About a third. Not a dollar.

¶23 So no, this doesn't prove that airlines lose money flying. Delta doesn't report profit by source, so nobody can honestly tell you what share of its profit comes from Amex. What the numbers do show is the size of the cushion. Delta's CEO, Ed Bastian, calls the company's growing loyalty ecosystem "one of the most important drivers of our structural durability." In twenty twenty-six, fuel jumped, and the bank's payments kept growing.

¶24 This is what Pricewhy does. Every week, we take one price apart, line by line, with the sources on screen. If you want the next one, subscribe. There's a new breakdown every Tuesday and Friday.

**[STOP 5 — The vault: worth more than the airline?]**

¶25 So what is a program like that worth on its own? In twenty twenty, the airlines borrowed against them, and we got a rare look. [pause] United valued MileagePlus at about twenty-one point nine billion dollars, its own estimate, and raised six point eight billion against it. At the time, according to a Financial Times analysis, the stock market valued all of United, planes included, at roughly ten billion.

¶26 An outside appraisal put American's program, AAdvantage, at nineteen and a half to thirty-one and a half billion dollars, while American's whole market value was reportedly under ten billion.

¶27 Two caveats, because this is where headlines go wrong. An appraisal is an estimate, not a price anyone paid. And market value is what's left for shareholders after debt. American, for example, had about thirty point seven billion dollars of net debt at the end of twenty twenty-five. So it's not like for like. As a Bank of America analyst told CNN, the programs and the airlines "can't exist without each other."

¶28 There is one real price tag, and it's in Canada. Our Price Board this time covers just the US and Canada, because that's where the comparable data is. In August twenty twenty-six, according to Skift and the Canadian Press, Air Canada sold a quarter of its Aeroplan program for two and a half billion Canadian dollars. That implies about ten billion Canadian for the whole program, around seven point two billion US. Skift puts Air Canada's own market value this year at five and a half to seven and a half billion Canadian.

¶29 Here's the strange part. In twenty nineteen, Air Canada had bought Aeroplan back for roughly four hundred fifty million Canadian dollars in cash, plus the points it owed members. Seven years later, a quarter of it sold at an implied ten billion. Skift's own caveat: the comparison "flatters Aeroplan," for the same reason as before. Debt.

**[LAST STOP — Who pays, and the verdict]**

¶30 Last stop: back at the coffee shop. Who actually pays for all those miles? [pause] According to Reuters, the National Retail Federation argues that premium cards carry the highest swipe fees, so shoppers who don't use them help subsidize the ones who do. Airlines reject that picture, and Alaska Airlines' loyalty chief says members without cards "continue to earn meaningful value through flying."

¶31 Washington is watching too. In September twenty twenty-four, the Department of Transportation demanded records from American, Delta, Southwest and United on their rewards programs, including devaluation and hidden pricing. According to Reuters, the replies are still under review. No findings have been published.

¶32 And in January twenty twenty-six, the Credit Card Competition Act was reintroduced. It would make large banks let stores route card payments over a second network, to push swipe fees down. Its co-author, Senator Dick Durbin, said in twenty twenty-four: "Modern day airlines have become credit card companies that also happen to own airplanes." Airlines warned, in a letter reported by Reuters, that the bill "could force them to stop offering rewards credit cards." As of November twenty twenty-six, there's been no Senate vote.

¶33 So, where did your dollar go? [pause] The store paid a swipe fee to the bank. The bank bought miles, perks and a brand from the airline. The airline counted part of it right away and parked the rest until you fly. And a year of those swipes added up to a check bigger than Delta's entire pre-tax profit.

¶34 In the air, or in your wallet? Both. The Amex cash was equal to about fourteen percent of Delta's revenue in twenty twenty-five, so most of the money still comes from somewhere else. But flying runs on thin margins, about two cents on the dollar this year, by IATA's forecast. The bank's check is the cushion under those margins, and it keeps growing. No wonder Delta's CEO is "really reticent to put a third party, a financial investor between us and our best customers."

¶35 One thing worth knowing, as information, not advice: by the time you earn a mile on one of these cards, the airline has already been paid for it. Whether any card makes sense for you depends on your own numbers. We don't recommend cards.

¶36 Airlines aren't the only business where the real product is a card in your wallet. Costco runs its own version of this trick, with a membership card and a hot dog whose price never moves. That one's next, right here.
