## Box-office split explained

**1. What the companies say (primary sources):**
- **Cinemark 10-K (FY2025).** "The majority of film rental rates are negotiated on a sliding scale formula." Under a sliding scale, "we pay a percentage of box office revenues using a pre-determined scale that is based upon box office performance of the film for its full theatrical run." Under "firm terms," the percentage "can either be an aggregate rate for the full theatrical run or rates that decline over the term of the theatrical run." The rate can also be a settlement negotiated after the run. (proven)
- **AMC 10-K (FY2025).** Film licenses are "film-by-film and theatre-by-theatre." Rental fees are "based on the box office performance of each film." Less often, the fee is "a mutually agreed settlement rate that is fixed." In some European territories, theaters pay a flat amount per ticket. (proven)
- **Blockbusters cost theaters a higher percentage.** AMC explains its lower 2025 film-cost rate by "concentration of box office revenues … in lower grossing films … which typically results in lower film exhibition costs." Cinemark: "Film rental costs as a percentage of revenue are generally higher for periods in which more blockbuster films are released." (proven)
- **Example:** AMC's U.S. film cost was 51.2% for FY2025 and rose to 52.9% in blockbuster-heavy Q3 2026. (proven)

**2. Actual averages (what theaters really pay over a year):**
- **AMC U.S.:** 51.2% of admissions, so AMC keeps about **49 cents of each ticket dollar** before any other cost. (proven)
- **Cinemark U.S.:** 58.0% of admissions goes to "film rentals **and advertising**." Cinemark keeps about **42 cents** before other costs. Cinemark does not break advertising out, so pure film rent is somewhat lower than 58%. (proven)
- **International is lower:** AMC 38.5%, Cinemark 51.4%. (proven)

**3. Reported deal terms for big releases:**
- WSJ (Nov 2017, via Dow Jones): most releases send "about 55%" to studios, and major hits average "about 60%." Disney's terms for *The Last Jedi* were about 65%, plus a 4-week largest-auditorium requirement and a possible extra 5% for violations. (probable)
- LA Times via UPI (Apr 2013): Disney "sought as much as 65 percent" on *Iron Man 3*, versus the typical 50–55%. AMC settled; terms were not disclosed. (probable)

**4. Opening weeks vs later weeks (how it used to work, and still sometimes does):**
- Academic evidence:
  - **Filson, Switzer & Besocke (2005), *Economic Inquiry* 43(2):354–369** studied 2,769 contracts from a 13-theater St. Louis chain. Most were sliding-scale deals in which the distributor's share declines over the weeks of a run; Gil & Lafontaine (2012) report the figure as 89%. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ecinqu/v43y2005i2p354-369.html (proven that the paper exists; the 89% figure is probable, seen via abstracts and citing papers)
  - **Gil & Lafontaine (2012), *Journal of Industrial Economics* 60(2):187–219:** "revenue-sharing terms go down with weeks since release." https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jindec/v60y2012i2p187-219.html (probable)
- Industry analyst Doug Stone (Box Office Analyst; HuffPost contributor, Apr 2015):
  - Older declining scales ran roughly 70% / 60% / 50% for weeks 1/2/3.
  - Current deals are often an aggregate percentage tied to total domestic gross, e.g. about 55% at roughly $100M and 60% at $200M.
  - He calls the popular "studios take 90%" belief a misconception.
  - https://www.huffpost.com/entry/why-are-popcorn-candy-and_b_7119772 (uncertain: a single analyst, illustrative numbers)
- **Script-safe framing:** "Studios typically take around half of every ticket dollar over a year. On the biggest blockbusters, reported terms have reached about 60–65%. Many contracts start with a higher studio share that falls the longer a film plays."

**5. Practical consequence (the theater's math):**
- If a theater raises a ticket by $1, it keeps only about 42–49 cents (U.S. averages above). (calc)
- If it raises popcorn by $1, it keeps the whole dollar, because the ingredients cost the same. (calc; logical inference from the 10-K cost structure)

## Popcorn cost per serving

**Bottom line: no credible, current, itemized cost for a large tub (kernels, oil, salt, topping, bucket) was found.** Chains do not disclose popcorn-specific costs. The only hard, current number is the cost of all food and beverage together.

**Proven (company-wide, all concession items combined):**

| Chain / segment | Product cost per $1 of concession sales | Implied gross margin | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| AMC U.S. | 18.1 cents | 81.9% | FY2025 |
| Cinemark U.S. | 18.9 cents | 81.1% | FY2025 |
| AMC consolidated | 19.6 cents | 80.4% | FY2025 |
| Cinemark consolidated | 19.6 cents | 80.4% | FY2025 |
| AMC U.S. | 16.2 cents | 83.8% | Q3 2026 |

- These ratios include sodas, candy, hot food, alcohol and merchandise, and they exclude labor.
- Cinemark says merchandise and "enhanced food options" are "lower margin items." Its 2025 concession cost rate rose partly because of "a higher mix of merchandise." Popcorn's own cost ratio is therefore very likely below the average, but no company publishes it.

**Older estimates of popcorn alone (use only with attribution and date):**
- **Richard McKenzie, UC Irvine economist,** author of *Why Popcorn Costs So Much at the Movies* (Springer/Copernicus, 2008): https://www.springer.com/us/book/9780387769998
  - Told ABC News (Jul 2008) that popcorn "must cost less than 10 cents an ounce." He also admitted the real costs "are trade secrets." https://abcnews.com/Entertainment/story?id=5379179 (uncertain)
  - The Hustle cites McKenzie (via CNN) for "around $0.90 to produce a bag," and calls that a 788% markup on a $7.99 medium. https://thehustle.co/the-economics-of-movie-theater-popcorn (uncertain: secondary, old)
- **Marketplace (Sep 9, 2008):** the host says "a big tub of popcorn costs about 60 cents." McKenzie: "once you get two refills… down to less than 8 cents an ounce." https://www.marketplace.org/story/2008/09/09/why-does-movie-popcorn-cost-so-much (uncertain)
- **Script-safe phrasing:** "Economists have estimated the raw ingredients of a tub at well under a dollar, though theaters don't publish the real number. What we can see in the filings: across everything at the snack counter, AMC paid about 18 cents in product cost for every dollar it took in."

## Surprising facts (pattern interrupts)

1. **The theater is a bank for your ticket money.** AMC collects admissions in cash up front, but film rentals "are ordinarily paid to distributors from 20 to 45 days following receipt." AMC calls this an operating "float." (AMC FY2025 10-K; proven)
2. **AMC sells a popcorn subscription.** The AMC Popcorn Pass costs $29.99 plus tax per year and gives 50% off one large popcorn per day (Dec 1, 2025 – Dec 31, 2026; in-theatre only, not mobile order). A Stubs member who uses it every day buys popcorn at half price, and AMC still sells 365 large-popcorn transactions to that member. ([AMC press release, Nov 25, 2025](https://investor.amctheatres.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/404/amc-theatres-launching-amc-popcorn-pass-giving-amc-stubs-moviegoers-half-price-large-popcorn-every-day-through-the-end-of-2026-beginning-cyber-monday-december-1); proven. A 2027 pass at $29.99 has been reported on a deal forum: https://slickdeals.net/f/20248836-2027-amc-popcorn-pass-29-99 (uncertain)
3. **AMC is also a grocery-store popcorn brand.** "AMC Theatres Perfectly Popcorn" is sold ready-to-eat and microwaveable in grocery stores and on Amazon. The 10-K describes it as entering "the multi-billion dollar popcorn industry." (AMC 10-K; proven)
4. **The $25–$80 buckets are big business, but not the highest-margin item.**
   - Prices: *Dune: Part Two* sandworm $24.99; R2-D2 combo $49.99; *Deadpool & Wolverine* $29.99 (CNBC, Feb 2025). The *Fantastic Four* "Galactus" bucket cost $80 and holds "more than twice" a normal large (NPR, Jul 2025: https://www.wunc.org/2025-07-13/popcorn-buckets-are-the-new-frontier-in-movie-branding-and-fans-are-eating-it-up).
   - AMC merchandise was $54M in 2023 (about 3% of F&B revenue) and about $65M in 2024.
   - Cinemark's 10-K calls merchandise a "lower margin" item, and says a "higher mix of merchandise" pushed its 2025 concession cost rate up. The viral bucket earns more dollars but a lower margin percentage than plain popcorn. (probable / proven)
5. **Half of AMC's U.S. audience is in its loyalty program.** Stubs members were about 51% of U.S. attendance in 2025 (proven). A-List subscribers alone were about 20% of U.S. attendance per the CEO in 2026 (probable, Fortune). At Cinemark, Movie Club subscribers drive 30% of domestic admissions revenue (proven).
6. **AMC tried pricing seats like airline seats, and dropped it.** In Feb 2023 AMC launched "Sightline": center seats cost more and front rows cost less. TheWrap reports AMC ended it after testing at three locations. Frequent moviegoers kept buying the pricier seats, but few people wanted the discounted front rows. In Feb 2026 AMC said it would instead block the best seats for A-List and Premiere members at no extra charge. (TheWrap, Feb 24, 2026: https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/business/amc-stubs-reserved-seats/ ; probable)
7. **One good summer flips the profit-and-loss statement.** AMC went from a -$298.2M net loss in Q3 2025 to +$109.3M net earnings in Q3 2026 on 28% more attendance. Theaters' costs are mostly fixed, so attendance is everything. (AMC 10-Q; proven)
8. **Seven studios control most of the screen.** Films from AMC's seven largest distributors made up about 83% of its U.S. admissions revenue in 2025. (AMC 10-K; proven) This explains the studios' negotiating power.

## Nuance and counterpoints

**Thesis test verdict.** The thesis was: "Theaters keep only a small part of the ticket because studios take a big share, so they make their real profit on concessions."
- **Mostly supported, but "small part" overstates it.**
- On average, U.S. theaters keep about **42–49 cents per ticket dollar** before other costs: AMC 48.8%, Cinemark 42.0%. Studios take a bigger share on blockbusters, with reported terms of 60–65%.
- Concessions keep **about 81–82 cents per dollar** after product costs.
- Result: concessions are about 34–40% of revenue but about **41–51% of what's left after paying studios and suppliers**:
  - AMC consolidated: F&B $1,344.3M vs tickets $1,377.6M, about equal.
  - Cinemark consolidated: concessions 49.4% of the three-line gross margin.
  - Cinemark U.S.: 51.3%.
- "Real profit on concessions" is fair as "the concession stand earns roughly as much or more than the ticket." It is not true that concessions are "all the profit," and theaters overall earn thin margins or lose money.

**Arithmetic for the verdict (FY2025, $M):**
- AMC consolidated:
  - Tickets net of film costs: 2,652.8 − 1,275.2 = 1,377.6
  - F&B net of product costs: 1,671.3 − 327.0 = 1,344.3
  - Other revenue: 524.8
  - Total 3,246.7; F&B share = 41.4%; tickets = 42.4%
- Cinemark consolidated:
  - Tickets: 1,544.7 − 877.0 = 667.7
  - Concessions: 1,227.2 − 240.5 = 986.7
  - Other: 343.1
  - Total 1,997.5; concessions share = 49.4%
- Cinemark U.S.:
  - Tickets: 1,266.0 − 733.8 = 532.2
  - Concessions: 998.2 − 188.3 = 809.9
  - Other: 238.0
  - Total 1,580.1; concessions share = 51.3%

**Counterpoints and caveats to include:**
1. **The concession stand is not free money. Fixed costs are huge.**
   - Cinemark U.S. per patron, FY2025: salaries $2.85, facility lease $2.03, utilities & other $3.14, G&A $1.56.
   - Cinemark: leases have "base terms generally ranging from 10 to 25 years," and facility lease expense is "primarily a fixed cost."
   - AMC rent = 18.3% of revenue.
   - (proven)
2. **Theaters are not getting rich.**
   - Cinemark net margin was 4.5% in FY2025.
   - AMC lost $632.4M in FY2025. Most of that is debt, not popcorn: $530.2M interest expense plus $196.0M net losses on debt extinguishment from refinancing.
   - AMC U.S. operating income was only $52.4M on $3.7B revenue.
   - (proven)
3. **The "80%+ margin" excludes labor.** "Food and beverage costs" and "concession supplies" are product costs. Staff wages sit in separate lines. (proven from 10-K line structure)
4. **Why not just raise ticket prices?**
   - (a) **The studio takes its percentage of any increase.** Of an extra $1 on a ticket, AMC U.S. keeps about 49 cents and Cinemark U.S. about 42 cents (calc). Of an extra $1 on popcorn, the theater keeps nearly all of it.
   - (b) **Price discrimination (Gil & Hartmann, *Marketing Science* 2009):** concession spending rises with how much a customer values moviegoing. Cheap-ish tickets bring in price-sensitive viewers, while high snack prices charge the enthusiasts more. Hartmann: "If you want to bring more consumers into the market, you need to keep ticket prices lower." (probable via Stanford GSB summary; the paper record is proven)
   - (c) **Attendance is fragile.** Even in the strong 2026 box office year, attendance through July was down 27% vs 2019 (Placer.ai via Reuters). S&P's Wade Holden: exhibitor revenue growth has been "mostly driven by increases in average ticket price." (probable)
   - (d) **Shorter streaming windows** give people a reason to wait. Cinemark's 10-K flags "reduction of exclusive theatrical windows" as a risk. (proven)
5. **Theaters have a third income stream that the thesis ignores:** screen advertising, online ticket fees, loyalty fees, gift-card breakage and more. AMC "other theatre" revenue was $524.8M (10.8% of revenue, calc). Cinemark "other" was $343.1M (11.0%). AMC books online ticketing fees in "other," not admissions. (proven)
6. **The split is not fixed per film.** Big hits cost theaters a higher percentage, but they bring far more people (and popcorn buyers). WSJ 2017: "a theater receiving 35% of ticket sales on a hit that grosses $700 million is in better shape than one receiving 50% on a $200 million movie." (probable)
7. **A contrarian economist.** McKenzie's 2008 book argues theater popcorn is only "slightly overpriced." The press release says the price is "lower than the cost of home-produced popcorn for most moviegoers" when you count their time and hassle, and it links the pricing to the 1948 antitrust decision. (EurekAlert, May 28, 2008: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/752595 ; uncertain: the mechanism is only in the book) Another useful McKenzie quote, to ABC News in 2008: "Many theaters consider themselves in the concession business, not the movie business." (probable)
8. **The averages hide the U.S./International gap.** Cinemark's international ticket averages $3.83 and its international concession spend $3.15. Use **U.S. segment** numbers for a U.S. audience. (proven)

## Do NOT claim

- **"Studios take 90% of the ticket" or "theaters only get 10%."** The 90/10 idea comes from an old "house nut" contract formula. Company filings show U.S. averages of 51–58% to film costs. The 70%+ figures quoted in 2008–2014 interviews (McKenzie, Oberleitner) are outdated and are not averages.
- **"Studios take X% in week 1, Y% in week 2…" as current fact.** Specific weekly percentages (70/60/50, 90/10) come from older contracts, an analyst's illustration or Spanish data. Only the *structure* (declining or sliding scales) is verified.
- **Disney's split on *The Force Awakens* (64%), *Avengers: Endgame* or *Avatar*.** Not verified. Only *The Last Jedi* (about 65%, WSJ 2017) and *Iron Man 3* ("sought as much as 65%," LA Times 2013) are reported by reputable outlets.
- **An itemized cost of a large popcorn** (kernels, oil, salt, topping, bucket) or a single "it costs X cents" figure stated as fact. Nothing current or itemized was found. The 60–90 cent figures are 2008-era estimates; always attribute and date them.
- **"Popcorn has an 85% (or 90%, 1,000%+) margin."** The 80%+ margin applies to *all* concessions' product cost, excluding labor, not to popcorn specifically. "85% profit / 46% of profits" (Time 2012, via Smithsonian) is dated and its method is unknown.
- **"Popcorn is the single most profitable item"** with a number. It is plausible, but no company discloses item-level margins.
- **A national price for a large popcorn at AMC or Cinemark.** Prices vary by location; only anecdotes (about $10–11) were found. If you show a menu price, photograph or screenshot a specific theater's menu and say so on screen.
- **AMC Popcorn Pass at $17.99.** One search snippet showed this, but the primary press release says $29.99 plus tax. Use $29.99.
- **"AMC loses money because of popcorn or ticket splits."** AMC's FY2025 loss is driven by debt: $530.2M interest and $196.0M net debt-extinguishment losses.
- **"Americans ate three times more popcorn in WWII"** as fact. It is an industry-group (Popcorn Board) claim with no data. If used, say "according to the Popcorn Board."
- **The exact year of the first popcorn machine.** Sources differ (1885 vs the 1893 Exposition). Say "in the 1880s and 90s."
- **Statements that studios get a cut of online ticket fees** (unverified) or of concession sales (no evidence; theaters keep concession revenue).
- **The Hustle's 2018 Cinemark figures** ($1.1B concessions, $181M cost). They are secondary and superseded; use the FY2025 10-K.
- **"Concessions are 20% of revenue and 40% of profits"** as a current U.S. fact. That comes from Spanish theater data in a 2009 paper. Current U.S. chains: concessions are 34–40% of revenue.
- **Any statistic attributed to "NATO" or "Cinema United" for 2025 average ticket price.** No official 2025 figure was found. The Numbers' $11.31 is a modeled estimate.
