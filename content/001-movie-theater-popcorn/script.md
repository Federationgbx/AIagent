## VOICE-READY narration (paste into the voice tool, one ¶ at a time)

**[HOOK]**

¶1 Eight dollars and thirty cents. [pause] That's what the average visitor to a Cinemark theater in the US spent at the snack bar in twenty twenty-five. The actual stuff behind that number — the popcorn, the drinks, the candy — cost the theater about a dollar fifty-seven.

¶2 So the theater is ripping you off at the counter. That's the story everyone tells. But it's the wrong place to look. The same visitor paid ten fifty-two for the average ticket. And six dollars and ten cents of that went straight back out the door — mostly to the studio.

¶3 By the end of this video, you'll know who takes what, why the popcorn costs what it costs, and how much of your whole night out the theater actually gets to keep. [pause] It's less than you think. Let's follow the money.

**[CHAPTER 1 — First stop: the studio]**

¶4 First stop: the ticket. Who actually gets your ticket money? [pause] You've probably heard that studios take ninety percent, and theaters live on scraps. The annual reports say otherwise.

¶5 In twenty twenty-five, AMC's film costs in the US — which include some advertising — came to fifty-one point two percent of its ticket sales. So AMC kept about forty-nine cents of each ticket dollar, before any other costs. At Cinemark, film rentals and advertising together took fifty-eight percent in the US. Cinemark doesn't split out the advertising, so the studios' real share is a bit lower. Either way, it's roughly half. Not ninety.

¶6 That's the first line on our receipt. Of the ten fifty-two ticket, six ten went to film rent and advertising. Four forty-two stayed with the theater.

¶7 And the split moves. Both chains say blockbusters cost them a bigger share. AMC's US film costs rose to fifty-two point nine percent in the hit-heavy third quarter of twenty twenty-six. Most of Cinemark's deals use a sliding scale tied to how the film performs, and many contracts start with a higher studio share that falls the longer a film plays. In twenty seventeen, the Wall Street Journal reported that most releases sent about fifty-five percent to the studio, and major hits about sixty. Disney's terms for Star Wars: The Last Jedi were reportedly about sixty-five.

¶8 Why can studios ask that much? In twenty twenty-five, films from AMC's seven biggest distributors brought in about eighty-three percent of its US ticket revenue. That's a lot of bargaining power on one side of the table.

¶9 And here's the part almost nobody reads. You pay for your ticket up front. But AMC's annual report says film rentals are "ordinarily paid to distributors from twenty to forty-five days following receipt." For a few weeks, the theater is holding the studio's money. AMC calls it a float.

¶10 Quick question before the next stop. [pause] What's the most you've ever spent at a movie theater snack bar? Tell us in the comments. We read them.

**[CHAPTER 2 — Second stop: the snack bar]**

¶11 Second stop: the snack bar. So what does the popcorn actually cost? [pause] Here's the honest answer: nobody publishes the cost of one tub. Not AMC. Not Cinemark. But both annual reports do publish the cost of everything sold at the counter, combined.

¶12 In twenty twenty-five, AMC paid about eighteen cents in product cost for every dollar of food and drink it sold in the US. Cinemark paid about nineteen cents. Flip that around: the theater keeps roughly eighty-one to eighty-two cents of every snack-bar dollar. And that's before paying anyone to work the counter — wages are a separate line.

¶13 That's line two on our receipt. Eight thirty at the snack bar. A dollar fifty-seven for the product. Six seventy-three stays with the theater.

¶14 And that average includes the expensive extras. Cinemark's annual report calls merchandise and enhanced food options "lower margin items." Collectible buckets are merchandise. One Fantastic Four bucket in twenty twenty-five reportedly cost eighty dollars. Buckets like that bring in more dollars, but a smaller share of each dollar is margin. So plain popcorn very likely costs the theater even less per dollar than the average. No chain says how much less.

**[CHAPTER 3 — Why the popcorn, and not the ticket]**

¶15 Which raises the obvious question. If the theater keeps so little of the ticket, why not just charge more for it? [pause] Do the math from the theater's side. Add one dollar to a ticket, and the studio takes its percentage of that dollar too. AMC keeps about forty-nine cents of it. Cinemark, about forty-two. Add one dollar to the popcorn, and the corn doesn't cost a cent more. The theater keeps nearly the whole dollar.

¶16 There's a second reason, and economists have measured it. A two thousand nine study in the journal Marketing Science, by Ricard Gil and Wesley Hartmann, found that people who value moviegoing more also spend more at the snack bar. So a lower ticket price brings in the price-sensitive crowd, and pricier snacks collect a bit more from the fans. In Hartmann's words: "If you want to bring more consumers into the market, you need to keep ticket prices lower."

¶17 Theaters learned this long ago. According to Smithsonian magazine, a Dallas chain in the nineteen thirties put popcorn machines in eighty of its theaters — but kept them out of its five best ones. Within two years, the popcorn theaters' profits soared. The other five went into the red. By nineteen forty-five, over half the popcorn eaten in America was eaten at the movies.

¶18 Or, as UC Irvine economist Richard McKenzie told ABC News in two thousand eight: "Many theaters consider themselves in the concession business, not the movie business."

**[CHAPTER 4 — The twist: what's actually left]**

¶19 So the snack bar is a money machine, and the theater is getting rich. Right? [pause] Let's total the receipt. From the ticket and the snacks together, Cinemark kept eleven dollars and sixteen cents per US visitor in twenty twenty-five. Six seventy-three of that came from the snack bar. About sixty percent — from the smaller of the two purchases.

¶20 Add the theater's third income stream — screen ads, online ticket fees and the like. The snack bar is still fifty-one percent of everything Cinemark's US theaters kept after paying studios and suppliers. At AMC worldwide, it's forty-one percent — about level with tickets. In other words: the snack bar earns the theater as much as the ticket does, or more.

¶21 This is what Pricewhy does. Every week, we take one price apart, line by line, with the sources on screen. If you want the next one, subscribe. Next up: how airlines really make money.

¶22 Now, here's the part that doesn't fit the story. That eleven sixteen isn't profit. The theater still has to pay for the building, the people and the power. Cinemark's leases generally run ten to twenty-five years, and it calls rent "primarily a fixed cost." The rent is due whether the movie is a hit or a flop.

¶23 So the receipt keeps going. Per US visitor: plus a dollar ninety-eight from ads, fees and other income. Minus two eighty-five for wages. Minus two oh three for rent. Minus three fourteen for utilities and everything else it takes to run the building. Minus a dollar fifty-six for the head office. Plus twenty-nine cents of other items. What's left: three dollars and eighty-four cents. And that's before depreciation, interest and taxes.

¶24 Pause the video and guess. After all of it — the studio, the product, the staff, the rent, the interest and the taxes — how much did Cinemark keep per visitor in twenty twenty-five? [pause] About seventy-three cents. That one is company-wide, because interest and taxes are only reported for the whole company. Cinemark's net profit margin that year: four and a half percent.

**[CHAPTER 5 — Last stop: AMC's debt]**

¶25 And then there's AMC. In twenty twenty-five, AMC lost six hundred thirty-two point four million dollars. So was it the popcorn? [pause] Follow the dollar one more time.

¶26 Per US visitor, after the studio, the snacks, the staff, the rent and the wear and tear on its theaters, AMC's US business made an operating profit of about thirty-four cents. Thin, but positive. Then came the debt. Interest: about two ninety-seven per visitor. Refinancing losses and other costs: another ninety-nine cents. After a few smaller items, AMC ended at minus three forty-four for every US visitor.

¶27 Company-wide, the picture is the same. AMC's interest expense in twenty twenty-five was five hundred thirty point two million dollars. It booked another one hundred ninety-six million in net losses from refinancing debt. Its corporate borrowings at the end of the year: about four billion dollars. The popcorn isn't the problem. The debt is.

¶28 To be fair to AMC, one good season changes a lot. In the third quarter of twenty twenty-six, with twenty-eight percent more visitors than a year earlier, AMC swung from a loss of two hundred ninety-eight point two million dollars to a profit of one hundred nine point three million. When most of your costs are fixed, every extra visitor matters.

**[CHAPTER 6 — The verdict]**

¶29 So, the verdict. Is the theater ripping you off? Not where you think. Of a ten fifty-two ticket, six ten went back out the door, mostly to the studio. The snack bar is where the theater makes its money: about eighty-one cents of every dollar, before staff. And that money pays for the rent, the wages and the lights. After everything, Cinemark kept about seventy-three cents per visitor. AMC lost money — mostly because of its debt, not its popcorn.

¶30 One thing worth knowing — information, not advice. Roughly half of your ticket goes to whoever made the film. Most of your snack-bar dollar stays with the theater down the street. So when you skip the popcorn, the studio doesn't notice. The theater does.

¶31 Movie theaters aren't the only business where the money isn't made on the thing you came for. Airlines have an even stranger business model — that one's right here.
