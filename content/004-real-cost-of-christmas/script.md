## VOICE-READY narration (paste into the voice tool, one ¶ at a time)

**[HOOK]**

¶1 This is a partridge in a pear tree. In twenty twenty-five, it cost four hundred twenty dollars. [pause] The bird was twenty. The tree was four hundred. Buy all twelve days from the song, and the bill comes to fifty-one thousand, four hundred seventy-six dollars.

¶2 That's a tongue-in-cheek price index a bank has kept since nineteen eighty-four. But the real thing is no joke: America's holiday shopping season is forecast to top one trillion dollars this year. [pause] And here's what doesn't fit. The government's official price index for toys has fallen about seventy percent since the year two thousand. So why does Christmas feel more expensive every year? Let's walk through the season, date by date, and keep the receipt.

**[NOVEMBER 1 — The meter starts]**

¶3 November first. That's the day the National Retail Federation starts counting. Its forecast for this November and December: one point oh one three trillion dollars, up four point two percent. Last year's season came in at nine hundred seventy-two billion.

¶4 Now, the part almost nobody reads. That trillion is not Christmas gifts. It's nearly everything sold by retailers for two whole months. Only car dealers, gas stations and restaurants are left out. So your groceries are in there too. And about half of the growth the federation expects comes from higher prices, not from people buying more. That's according to its own chief economist.

¶5 So what does one person actually plan to spend? Last year, the federation's survey said eight hundred ninety dollars and forty-nine cents. About six hundred twenty-eight of that for gifts. The rest for food, decorations and cards. Gallup asks a simpler question: how much will you spend on gifts? This October, the answer was one thousand and eleven dollars.

¶6 First line on our receipt: a gift budget of about a thousand dollars. Remember that number. By Christmas Eve, it's going to look very different.

**[THANKSGIVING WEEKEND — The rush]**

¶7 Thanksgiving weekend. The federation expects one hundred eighty-two point seven million people to shop. Sixty-four percent say they'll do most of their holiday shopping then. Five years ago, it was fifty-one percent. And the most-wanted gift this year? Gift cards, at forty-seven percent. Keep that one in mind too.

¶8 It's also when the tree goes up. In the growers' latest published survey, Americans bought about twenty-one point six million real trees in twenty twenty-three. The median price: seventy-five dollars. For something that, according to the growers' association, can take up to a decade to grow. [pause] Quick question before we go on: real tree or fake, and what did yours cost? Tell us in the comments. We read them.

¶9 Receipt line two: the weekend when most of that budget gets spent. So where does the money go once December starts? Let's start with the song.

**[DECEMBER — The twelve days]**

¶10 Since nineteen eighty-four, PNC, the bank, has priced every gift in "The Twelve Days of Christmas", as a light-hearted cousin of the government's consumer price index. The twenty twenty-five total: fifty-one thousand, four hundred seventy-six dollars and twelve cents. Up four point five percent. Official inflation, by the bank's own comparison, was three percent.

¶11 And if your true love bought every gift every time it's sung, all three hundred sixty-four of them, the bill would be two hundred eighteen thousand, five hundred forty-three dollars. [pause] Now read that bill line by line. The birds, from the partridge to the swans: about thirty-one percent. The pear tree and the gold rings: four percent. The other sixty-five percent is people. Maids, ladies, lords, pipers and drummers.

¶12 The gold rings jumped thirty-two and a half percent, the biggest increase on the list. Gold itself rose even more. But pause the video and guess: which gift has not changed price since two thousand nine? [pause] The eight maids-a-milking. Fifty-eight dollars. PNC prices them at the federal minimum wage: eight maids, one hour each, at seven dollars and twenty-five cents. And the federal minimum wage hasn't moved since July twenty-fourth, two thousand nine.

¶13 The swans are the opposite story. Seven swans-a-swimming: thirteen thousand, one hundred twenty-five dollars. A quarter of the entire bill. By PNC's own records, the same seven swans cost thirteen thousand, one hundred twenty-one dollars in nineteen eighty-four. Four dollars more, in forty-one years.

¶14 The lords-a-leaping went the other way. Ten of them cost two thousand nine hundred three dollars in nineteen eighty-four. In twenty twenty-five, sixteen thousand, eight hundred thirty-six. Almost six times as much. Receipt line three: the twelve days, fifty-one thousand, four hundred seventy-six dollars, and two-thirds of it is paying people.

¶15 So is the song really more expensive than in nineteen eighty-four? By PNC's count, the twelve gifts cost about twenty thousand dollars back then. Adjusted for inflation, that's about sixty-two thousand in twenty twenty-five dollars. The twenty twenty-five bill is fifty-one. [pause] So after inflation, the song got cheaper. Unless you take out the swans. Without them, the rest of the list costs five and a half times what it did. And that's the clue.

**[THE TWIST — What actually got expensive]**

¶16 Because the twist isn't about birds. It's this: the stuff got cheaper. The people got expensive. [pause] Look at the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Its price index for toys was seventy-one percent lower in October twenty twenty-six than in October two thousand. Over the same years, prices overall rose ninety-four percent.

¶17 Put it in dollars. Toys that cost a hundred dollars in two thousand would cost about twenty-nine today. A hundred dollars of goods in general: about a hundred fifty-eight. A hundred dollars of services, the things people do for you: about two hundred twenty-one.

¶18 One honest caveat. Price indexes try to adjust for quality, so part of that drop is toys getting better for the money, not just smaller price tags. And since twenty twenty-four, toy prices have been rising again, about nine percent in two years. But the long-run pattern isn't an American quirk.

¶19 Here's our Price Board. We checked the official toy price indexes in all four of our countries, from two thousand to this autumn. In the UK, games, toys and hobbies are down about a quarter, while prices overall nearly doubled. In Canada, toys and games are down five percent. Everything else: up seventy-seven. In Australia, toys are down about eleven percent, while prices overall rose about a hundred and five. Each country measures differently, so compare the direction, not the size. And the direction is the same everywhere.

¶20 Economists have a name for this. Baumol's cost disease, after William Baumol and William Bowen, who described it in nineteen sixty-six. A string quartet still needs four musicians and the same time to play, just like it always did. Factories get faster. A quartet can't. But the musicians' pay still has to keep up with everyone else's. So anything made of human time gets relatively more expensive. Lords-a-leaping included.

¶21 Now go back to that budget. Gallup's gift budget was one thousand fourteen dollars in October twenty twenty-four. This October, one thousand eleven. Flat. But adjusted for inflation, the twenty twenty-four budget would be about one thousand eighty-four dollars today. So the typical gift budget has quietly shrunk, by about seven percent.

¶22 And last November, Americans cut their estimate to seven hundred seventy-eight dollars. The biggest mid-season drop Gallup has recorded. Yet the season's sales still grew three point one percent. So who's carrying the trillion? Higher prices. And, in the federation economist's words, higher-income households doing "more of the heavy lifting." [pause] Receipt line four: the stuff got cheaper, and the budget didn't grow.

¶23 If this kind of receipt is useful: every week, we take one price apart, line by line. Subscribe if you want the next one. On Friday, it's why Australia is so expensive.

**[DECEMBER 26 — The returns]**

¶24 December twenty-sixth. Retailers told the federation they expect seventeen percent of holiday sales to come back as returns. On last season's nine hundred seventy-two billion, that's roughly one hundred sixty-five billion dollars, by our math. And retailers estimate that nine percent of all returns are fraudulent.

¶25 Then there are the gifts that stay, but aren't loved. In nineteen ninety-three, Yale economist Joel Waldfogel surveyed his students and concluded, quote, "holiday gift-giving destroys between ten percent and a third of the value of gifts." In his two surveys, a gift that cost a hundred dollars was worth about sixty-six to eighty-seven dollars to the person who got it.

¶26 To be fair, later studies found the opposite. A broader survey found a median of a hundred eleven dollars of value per hundred spent. An experiment with real money at stake found a hundred thirty-five. Sentimental value counts. Maybe that's why forty-seven percent of shoppers ask for gift cards. Although, in a Bankrate survey from twenty twenty-four, forty-three percent of American adults were sitting on unused gift cards or store credit, averaging two hundred forty-four dollars. Receipt line five: seventeen percent, back to the store.

**[JANUARY — The bill]**

¶27 January. The statement arrives. In a LendingTree survey last December, thirty-seven percent of consumers said they took on holiday debt. The average: one thousand two hundred twenty-three dollars, the highest since twenty twenty-two. Credit cards were sixty-two percent of it. Buy now, pay later loans, thirty-five. And forty-one percent of those borrowers were still paying off the holiday before.

¶28 What does that cost? The Federal Reserve's average rate on credit card accounts paying interest was about twenty-two percent in August twenty twenty-six. At that rate, one thousand two hundred twenty-three dollars, paid off in twelve equal payments, adds about one hundred fifty-three dollars of interest. By our math, that's one more gift. For the bank. [pause] Receipt line six: the interest. A price you pay for time.

**[THE VERDICT]**

¶29 So, is Christmas getting more expensive? Partly. The season's total keeps setting records, and about half of this year's expected growth is just higher prices. But the typical gift budget has sat near a thousand dollars for three years, and after inflation it's smaller. And the things under the tree have, by the official numbers, gotten cheaper in all four countries we checked.

¶30 What got expensive is everything made of people and time. The lords-a-leaping. The service. The interest on January's bill. That's the real cost of Christmas: not the stuff, the hours. [pause] Which brings us back to the partridge. Twenty dollars for the bird. Four hundred for the tree. At Christmas, the expensive part is rarely the one you're looking at.

¶31 Next on Pricewhy: the same products, with much bigger price tags. Why is Australia so expensive? That one's on Friday, and it's right here.
