## The season, date by date (for a Timeline structure)

1. **Nov 1 — the meter starts.** NRF counts Nov 1–Dec 31; it forecasts $1.013T for 2026 (+4.2%) after $972B (+3.1%) in 2025. Definition matters: it is *all* retail except auto dealers, gas stations and restaurants — so it includes everyday shopping, not only gifts (NRF methodology in the release). About half of the expected 2026 growth is prices, half real spending (NRF chief economist).
2. **Thanksgiving weekend.** 182.7M expected shoppers; 64% plan to do most of their shopping then (51% five years ago); 43% start before November. Gift cards top wish lists (47%). The real tree: 21.58M bought in 2023, median $75 (NCTA).
3. **December — the 12 days.** PNC 2025: $51,476.12; True Cost $218,542.98; 65% performers, 31% birds, 4% things. Maids = federal minimum wage (unchanged since July 24, 2009). Swans flat since 1984 (+$4.14). Lords ×5.8.
4. **The twist.** Goods vs people: toys index −71% since 2000 while services +121% (US). Same direction in UK, Canada, Australia. Baumol's cost disease explains why human-time goods get relatively dearer. Gallup budget flat ($1,014 → $1,011) = ~7% smaller in real terms.
5. **Dec 26 — returns.** 17% of holiday sales expected back (≈ $165B on 2025's $972B). 9% of returns fraudulent (retailers). Waldfogel: gifts worth 66–87% of their price to recipients; later studies 111–135%.
6. **January — the bill.** 37% took on holiday debt averaging $1,223; 41% still paying last year's; at the Fed's 22.36% card rate, 12 equal payments ≈ $153 interest.

## Why "people got expensive" (Baumol)
- William J. Baumol & William G. Bowen, *Performing Arts: The Economic Dilemma* (1966, MIT Press); earlier AER article 1965. A live string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same time as ever, so its productivity can't rise; but its pay has to keep up with the rest of the economy, so its relative price rises ("cost disease"). Sources: CEPR/VoxEU https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/how-william-baumol-created-cultural-economics ; Chicago Booth Review https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/diagnosing-william-baumols-cost-disease — probable (concept description).
- Our data fit the pattern: PNC performers (lords ×5.8) vs swans (flat); BLS services +120.5% vs goods +58.2% vs toys −71% (Oct 2000 → Oct 2026). Some economists debate how far it applies to health and education (Booth Review) — irrelevant to our use.

## Surprising facts (pattern interrupts)
1. The partridge is $20.18; the pear tree it sits in is $400.
2. The only gift that hasn't gone up since 2009: eight maids-a-milking ($58 = 8 × $7.25 federal minimum wage, unchanged since July 24, 2009).
3. Seven swans: $13,120.86 in 1984, $13,125 in 2025 — $4.14 in 41 years. They are a quarter of the bill.
4. After inflation the 12 days are *cheaper* than in 1984 ($62,045 vs $51,476) — but only because of the swans; without them, ×5.6.
5. The official US toy price index is 71% lower than in Oct 2000; services cost 121% more.
6. The "trillion-dollar Christmas" is all store sales for two months, not gifts.
7. Gallup's November 2025 budget dropped $229 in one month — the biggest mid-season drop it has recorded — yet the season's sales still grew 3.1%.
8. A Yale economist (1993): gift-giving "destroys between 10 percent and a third of the value of gifts." Later studies: gifts are worth *more* than they cost (111%, 135%).

## Nuance and counterpoints
- The toy index is quality-adjusted (price indexes try to remove quality changes), so part of the long fall is better toys for the money, not only lower price tags. And US toy prices have risen again since 2024 (+9.1% in two years, faster than all items +6.9%).
- NRF's per-person survey and Gallup's are *intentions*, not receipts. Gallup's own sample error is ±4 points (±$78 on the spending estimate in 2025).
- The PNC index is a light-hearted index from a bank's wealth-management arm; its own items (swans, performers) are priced from a few suppliers. Use it as a story, not as an inflation measure.
- PNC's 1984 total has been restated over time (see Do NOT claim). We use PNC's current figure and say "by PNC's count".
- Returns 17% and fraud 9% are retailer expectations/estimates from a survey, not measured totals.
- LendingTree is a lender-comparison company; its survey is online, non-probability. Label "a LendingTree survey".
- Waldfogel's sample was Yale students (not representative); the counter-studies found the opposite. Present both.

**Thesis verdict:** partly true — the season's total keeps setting records and the PNC index beat official inflation in 2025, but about half of this year's expected growth is just higher prices, the typical gift budget has been flat for three years (≈7% smaller after inflation), and the stuff itself (toys) got cheaper in all four countries; what got expensive is anything made of people's time — and borrowing.

## Do NOT claim
- "Americans spend $1 trillion on Christmas gifts." NRF's $1.013T is a forecast of all retail sales in Nov–Dec (excl. cars, gas, restaurants). Gifts are a slice of it.
- "Holiday sales topped $1 trillion" — not yet: 2025 was $972B; 2026 is a forecast. (Coverage in 2025 also predicted the first $1T season — it didn't happen.)
- "The 12 Days of Christmas cost $12,623 in 1984." That was the figure reported in 2004 (UPI); PNC's own pages now give $20,023.57 for 1984. Use PNC's current number, attributed.
- "PNC's 2026 index is …" — not published as of Nov 21, 2026.
- "Toys cost 71% less at the store." It is a quality-adjusted price index; say "the official price index for toys fell about 71%".
- "Tariffs pushed toy prices up." Toy prices rose 9.1% (Oct 2024 → Oct 2026) but we have no source that isolates the cause.
- "83.9% average yield" for Waldfogel — that number appears in secondary coverage; the paper's Table 1 says 66.1% and 87.1%.
- "Waldfogel proved Christmas wastes $X billion today." His dollar estimates were for 1992 and based on Yale students.
- A 2024/2025 real-tree median price. NCTA has not published one we could read; the $80–$100 "average" in media is a range quote, not a survey result. Only "median $75 in 2023".
- "RCTB: 91% of growers won't raise prices in 2026" — seen only in a search summary; the page returned 403. Not used.
- "Top 10% of spenders = 70% of sales" — appeared in a search summary but not found on NRF's pages. Not used; use the exact NRF quote about higher-income households instead.
- Any per-person spending ranking of US/UK/Canada/Australia (definitions differ; see Price Board).
- Personal advice ("pay with cash", "avoid BNPL"). We only show the arithmetic.

1. Verdict: partly true — totals and the "12 days" rise, but the typical gift budget is flat (≈7% smaller after inflation) and toys got cheaper in all 4 countries; people's time and interest got expensive.
